What counts as a real pull-up

~4 min read

Video: full rep breakdown (full course)

Before you train it, agree on the target. A rep only counts when:

  • You start from a full dead hang — arms straight, shoulders active (not shrugged to your ears).
  • Your chin clears the bar without kipping, swinging, or jerking your knees up.
  • You lower under control back to a full hang. The lower half is where strength is built.

Grip is shoulder-width, palms facing away (pronated). A slightly narrower grip is fine while you build strength — it lets the bigger back muscles help.

Mindset

Progress here is measured in seconds and half-reps, not reps. Adding 5 seconds to a hang or one clean negative is a real session. Treat it that way and you'll get there in weeks.

Step 1 · The dead hang

Goal: 45 seconds, unbroken

Everything starts with being able to hold the bar. The dead hang builds grip, forearm and shoulder endurance, and teaches your shoulders to stay engaged under load.

How to do it: jump or step up to the bar, hang with straight arms, feet off the floor. Pull your shoulder blades slightly down and back — imagine tucking them into your back pockets — so you're not just dangling from your joints. Breathe. Hold.

Watch for

Sharp shoulder pinching means you're hanging fully passive. Re-engage: pull the shoulders away from your ears and the pinch usually disappears.

Step 2 · Scapular pulls

Goal: 3 × 8 slow reps

This is the missing link for most people. A scapular pull is a tiny pull-up powered only by your shoulder blades — your arms stay straight the whole time.

  1. Start in an active dead hang.
  2. Without bending your elbows, pull your shoulder blades down — your chest rises an inch or two and your body lifts slightly.
  3. Hold for 1 second at the top.
  4. Lower slowly back to a full hang. That's one rep.

If you can't feel your back working, you'll never own the bottom of a pull-up — which is exactly where beginners get stuck. Do these until the movement feels obvious.

Cue

"Bend the bar" — even though your arms are straight, imagine trying to snap the bar in half. That fires the lats and sets your shoulders correctly.

Step 3 · Negatives (eccentric reps)

Goal: 5 × 1 rep, 5-second lower

You're stronger lowering than lifting. Negatives use that to build the exact strength a full pull-up needs.

  1. Get your chin above the bar however you can — jump up, or use a box/chair.
  2. Hold the top position for 1 second, elbows bent, chest to bar.
  3. Lower yourself as slowly as possible — aim for a 5-count, no faster.
  4. Reset. Rest 90 seconds. Repeat.

When your 5 negatives all take a genuine 5 seconds each, you are physically capable of a pull-up. The next step just connects the dots.

Don't

Don't "fall" the first 80% and slow down at the end. The first few inches out of the top are the most valuable — control them.

Step 4 · Assisted reps → your first pull-up

Goal: reduce assistance to zero

Now train the concentric (the way up) with just enough help to do it cleanly:

  • Band-assisted: loop a resistance band over the bar and put a foot or knee in it. Use the lightest band that lets you do 5 clean reps.
  • Foot-assisted: a bar around chest height, feet on the floor, pushing through your legs only as much as you need.
  • Partner-assisted: a hand under your feet, giving minimal support.

Every week, reduce the help: a lighter band, less leg drive. The day the assistance is basically zero, test a solo rep from a dead hang. That's your first pull-up.

Quick check: your 5 negatives all take a full 5 seconds, but a solo pull-up still fails halfway up. What's the fix?

Do more dead hangs Add band-assisted reps and reduce the band weekly Start kipping to get past the sticking point

Your 3-week starter plan

3 sessions per week, ~15 min each

Do this on non-consecutive days (e.g. Mon / Wed / Fri). Warm up with 30 seconds of arm circles and one easy hang first.
