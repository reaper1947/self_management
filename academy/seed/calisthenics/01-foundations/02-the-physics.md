You do not need to enjoy physics to train. You do need four ideas, because every
plateau you will ever hit is one of them — and knowing which one turns "I'm
stuck" into "I know exactly what to change."

## 1. Torque is the only thing your shoulders feel

When you hold a static position, what your body resists is not your weight. It is
**torque** — the twisting effect of your weight about the joint holding you up.

![The lever-arm equation drawn on a hanging body](/academy/figures/lever-arm.svg)

```
τ = m · g · d
```

- **m** is your mass. You cannot change it quickly, and you should not try to.
- **g** is gravity. Not negotiable.
- **d** is the horizontal distance from the pivot to your **centre of mass**.

Two of the three are fixed. **d is the entire game.** Every progression in the
static half of this course is a way of shrinking d so you can train a position
you cannot yet hold, then letting it grow back as you get stronger.

This is why "just try the front lever" is useless advice and "tuck front lever,
8 seconds" is good advice. They are the same skill at two values of d.

## 2. Your centre of mass moves when you move

Your centre of mass is the mass-weighted average position of every part of you.
Standing upright it sits roughly behind your navel. Tuck your knees and it
travels toward your chest. Reach overhead and it rises.

That is the mechanism behind every tuck, straddle and half-lay in this course.
You are not doing an easier *version* of the skill. You are doing the same skill
with your centre of mass relocated.

Those percentages are computed, not estimated. The body model uses standard
segment masses and measures where the centre of mass actually lands in each

Now the part that catches people out:

![Where front-lever torque comes from, by body segment](/academy/figures/torque-breakdown.svg)

**Your torso alone is about a third of the total, and no amount of tucking
removes any of it.** A tuck can only fold away the legs. That is why even a deep
tuck still asks for roughly two-thirds of the full hold, and why a tuck front
lever is not a beginner move.

## 3. Difficulty does not scale with torque

If a tuck front lever is 65% of the torque of a full one, why does it feel
enormously easier — far more than 35% easier?

Because you are working near the top of your strength curve, and up there the
relationship between load and endurance is brutally steep. Drop the demand by a
third when you are at 95% of maximum and you might go from a 2-second hold to a
20-second one. The *torque* fell by a third. The *time you can hold it* went up
tenfold.

Two consequences, both of which shape how this course is written:

- **Small reductions in d buy large amounts of training volume.** That is why
  these ladders have so many rungs. Each is a modest change in leverage and a
  large change in how much quality work you can accumulate.
- **Small increases in d cost you just as steeply.** When you extend one leg out
  of a tuck and a 20-second hold collapses to 4, nothing has gone wrong. That is
  the curve behaving normally. Do not read it as failure.

## 4. Muscles are stronger at lowering than at lifting

A muscle produces more force while lengthening under load (**eccentric**) than
while holding (**isometric**), and more holding than while shortening
(**concentric**). Roughly:

![Relative force capacity: eccentric, isometric, concentric](/academy/figures/force-capacity.svg)

That gap is the most useful fact in this course, because it means:

**You can train a movement you cannot yet perform, by doing the lowering half of
it.**

Cannot do a pull-up? You can almost certainly lower yourself from the top slowly.
Cannot hold an advanced tuck? You can lower through it. Every "I can't do this
yet" in the syllabus has an eccentric answer, and that answer is usually the
fastest route to the concentric version.

It is also why eccentrics leave you sorer and need more recovery: more force
through the tissue, more micro-damage, more adaptation, more time.

## Putting the four together — a worked example

You want a front lever. You can hold a tuck for 15 seconds and an advanced tuck
for 3.

1. **Torque.** The advanced tuck is 76% of full, the tuck 65%. You are stuck on an
   11-percentage-point step.
2. **Centre of mass.** You do not have to take the whole step. Extend *one* leg
   halfway and you land between the rungs.
3. **The difficulty curve.** At 3 seconds you are far too close to your limit to
   accumulate useful volume. Back off to something you hold for 8–12.
4. **Eccentrics.** Add slow lowers *through* the advanced tuck to load the
   position you cannot yet hold.

That is the whole method. Everything after this is the method applied to specific
skills.
