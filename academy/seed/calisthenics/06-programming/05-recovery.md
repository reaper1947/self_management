Training is a request. Recovery is whether the request gets granted. This lesson
is the short, unglamorous version of what actually matters, with no supplements
and no hacks.

**A note on scope:** this is general training information, not medical or
dietary advice. If you have a health condition, are managing an injury, or are
changing your eating substantially, talk to a qualified professional. Nothing
here is a substitute for that.

## Sleep is the whole thing

If you change one input, change this one. Sleep is when most of the hormonal and
tissue-repair work happens, and no amount of anything else compensates for its
absence. Short sleep reliably reduces force output, slows skill learning, and —
most relevant here — impairs connective tissue repair, which is the slow link in
calisthenics.

The practical version, in order of impact:

1. **Consistent times.** Same bed and wake time, including weekends. Regularity
   matters more than the exact hours.
2. **Dark and cool.** Both genuinely change sleep quality.
3. **No screens in the last hour.** If that is unrealistic, dim everything.
4. **Caffeine has a long tail.** Half of a 14:00 coffee is still in you at 20:00.
5. **Train earlier if you can.** Hard training late can delay sleep onset.

If you are training hard and sleeping badly, **train less**. That is not a
compromise; it is the correct response.

## Eating, at the level that matters

Three things carry nearly all of the effect:

**Enough protein.** Tissue repair needs raw material. Spread it across the day
rather than in one large meal. If you eat plant-based, pay more attention to
variety and total amount.

**Enough total food.** Chronic under-eating is the most common reason a motivated
person stops progressing. The signs are recognisable: strength drifting down,
constant coldness, poor sleep, no appetite after training, injuries that take
forever. In calisthenics there is an extra trap — lower bodyweight genuinely makes
every skill easier, so being under-fuelled feels like it is working, right up
until it stops.

**Enough water.** Dull, and it moves performance more than most supplements.

Beyond those, details matter far less than consistency. There is no food that will
give you a front lever.

## Bodyweight, honestly

Every skill in this course is easier at a lower bodyweight, because every one of
them is `m · g · d` and you are m.

That is a true fact with a sharp edge on it. Losing fat helps, and is generally
good for you. Being persistently under-fuelled to chase a skill costs you tendon
health, sleep, strength and eventually the skill itself.

The honest framing: **do not diet your way to a front lever.** Train for it, eat
enough to recover from the training, and let bodyweight be an outcome.

## Load management across a week

Your tissue does not know which exercise caused the load. It knows the total. So:

- track **weekly straight-arm seconds** as one number
- track **weekly hard sets** for pushing and for pulling
- keep them within about 10% of last week

When life gets stressful, the total you can recover from drops even if your
training does not change. A hard week at work is a good week to train at 70%.

## Warm-up and cool-down

**Warm-up** is in the joint prep lesson, and it is not optional.

**Cool-down** is smaller than the internet suggests. Five minutes of easy hanging
and gentle movement is plenty. Long static stretching after hard training is fine
if you enjoy it and is not doing much for recovery.

## Signals to watch

| Signal | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| Resting heart rate up 5+ beats for several days | under-recovered |
| Grip strength down noticeably | systemic fatigue, a good early warning |
| Waking before the alarm, unable to sleep again | over-reaching |
| Joints ache in the morning | connective tissue fatigue — reduce volume now |
| No appetite after training | usually under-eating or over-training |
| Motivation gone for more than a few days | take the deload |
