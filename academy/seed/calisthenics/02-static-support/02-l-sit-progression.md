The L-sit is where the lever-arm idea stops being theory. It is one position with
a dial on it, and you turn the dial by straightening your knees.

## What it is

Supported on your hands — parallettes, dip bars, or the floor — with your legs
held straight out in front of you, parallel to the ground, making an L.

## The physics

![Torque about the hands in an L-sit](/academy/figures/l-sit.svg)

Your hands are the pivot. Your legs and torso hang off it, and the torque about
your shoulders is `m · g · d`, where d is the horizontal distance from your hands
to your centre of mass.

Tuck your knees and the mass of your legs sits close to the pivot. Extend them
and it travels away. Nothing about you has changed — not your strength, not your
weight. **The lever changed.**

So the ladder below is not four different exercises. It is one exercise at four
values of d, and knowing that tells you what to do when you get stuck: you do not
need a different movement, you need an intermediate d. Extending one leg halfway,
or bending one knee slightly, is always available to you.

The second demand is **compression** — actively holding the legs up, which is hip
flexor work, not just core work. Many people have the shoulder strength for an
L-sit and cannot get their legs level. That is a compression problem and it has
its own fix, below.

## The progression

| Rung | What to do | Target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Support hold** — prerequisite | 30 s |
| 2 | **Tuck L-sit** — knees to chest | 20 s |
| 3 | **Advanced tuck** — thighs parallel to floor, shins hanging | 20 s |
| 4 | **One-leg L-sit** — one leg straight, one tucked | 15 s each side |
| 5 | **Full L-sit** on parallettes | 15 s |
| 6 | **Full L-sit on the floor** — much harder, needs long arms or high compression | 10 s |
| 7 | **V-sit** — legs above parallel | 5 s |

Rung 6 surprises people. On the floor your shoulders are lower, so your legs must
clear the ground with nowhere to hang — the required compression jumps sharply.
It is normal to hold a solid L-sit on parallettes and fail completely on the
floor.

## The compression fix

If your shoulders are fine but your legs will not come up, train compression
directly, on a rest day or after your main work:

- **Seated compression lifts** — sit with legs straight, hands on the floor, lift
  the heels off the ground and hold 5 s. 5 reps.
- **Pike compression** — seated, pull your chest toward your thighs with your
  hands, then let go and hold the position with muscle for 5 s. 5 reps.
- **Hanging leg raises** — straight legs, slow, no swing. 3×8.

## Form cues

**Push the floor away.** Shoulders down, chest tall. A sunk shoulder shortens
nothing and costs everything.

**Knees locked, toes pointed.** A soft knee is a shorter lever, which means you
are training a rung below the one you think you are on.

**Lean back very slightly.** Not a slump — a small shift of the shoulders behind
the hands makes the legs come up. Too much and it becomes a different exercise.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Legs below parallel | compression, not strength | do the compression work above; hold the rung you own |
| Shoulders shrug up | losing the support position | go back to the support hold |
| Knees bend as fatigue arrives | at the edge of your rung | end sets earlier; quality over duration |
| Lower back rounds hard | trying to cheat compression by curling | tuck the pelvis deliberately, drop a rung |
| Can do it on bars, not the floor | expected | keep both in the plan, they progress separately |

## Programming it

Three times a week. **45–60 s total hold time** per session in clean sets. Add
about 10% per week to total time before you move a rung.

Pair it with compression work twice a week, never on the same day as heavy
lever work — the hip flexors are already busy there.

## Target

**15 seconds, full L-sit on parallettes, legs at or above parallel, knees
locked.** Then move to planche lean.
