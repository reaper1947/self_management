The planche is the hardest position in this course and the one most likely to
injure you if approached impatiently. It is also the purest demonstration of the
lever-arm rule, because the planche has a continuous dial rather than discrete
rungs: **lean**.

## What it is

Holding your body horizontal, face down, supported only on your hands, with
nothing touching the ground. The planche lean is the entry: hands on the floor,
feet still down, shoulders leaning forward past the hands.

## The physics

![Planche lean and the growing lever arm](/academy/figures/planche-lean.svg)

In a planche, your hands are the pivot and your centre of mass is **behind** them,
so your shoulders must produce a forward torque to stop you rotating backward.
The further you lean, the further your centre of mass travels past the hands, and
the larger d becomes.

That is what makes the planche special: **d is continuous**. You do not jump from
one rung to the next, you add a few degrees. This is enormously useful, because
it means you can always find a load you can hold — and dangerous, because it
means you can always find a load you cannot.

A planche also asks something the other statics do not: the load is on
**straight-arm shoulder flexion with the biceps tendon under tension at the
elbow**. That combination is exactly the one that produces biceps tendinopathy
and golfer's elbow when volume climbs too fast. The rules below are not caution
for its own sake.

## The progression

| Rung | What to do | Target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Plank** — perfect hollow, shoulders over hands | 60 s |
| 2 | **Planche lean, small** — shoulders just past the wrists | 30 s |
| 3 | **Planche lean, moderate** — shoulders clearly past, weight forward | 20 s |
| 4 | **Frog stand** — knees resting on the elbows, feet off | 30 s |
| 5 | **Tuck planche** — knees off the elbows, hips at shoulder height | 15 s |
| 6 | **Advanced tuck planche** — back flat, thighs vertical-ish | 10 s |
| 7 | **Straddle planche** | 5 s |

Most people should spend **months** on rungs 2 and 3. That is not a failure of
the plan, it is the plan. Straight-arm tissue adapts slowly, and the lean is
where it adapts safely.

## Measuring the lean

"Lean more" is not a number, so make it one. Set up in a plank with your hands on
a line, mark where your shoulders sit, and measure the horizontal distance from
wrist to shoulder. Write it down in centimetres.

Now "add lean" becomes "add one centimetre this week," which is a real, repeatable
progression instead of a vague instruction.

## Form cues

**Protract hard.** Push the floor away and let your upper back round — the
opposite of most gym advice, and correct here. A retracted scapula in a planche
puts the load onto the joint instead of the muscle.

**Posterior pelvic tilt.** Tuck the pelvis under. Without it the hips rise, the
lower back takes the strain, and the position becomes a different, easier,
uglier one.

**Elbows locked, inner elbow forward.** Non-negotiable. A bent-arm planche is a
different skill with a different injury profile.

**Fingers gripping the floor.** They are the brakes; they stop you tipping forward
and they teach the hand to participate.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Inner elbow pain | classic straight-arm overuse | stop, drop lean, halve the volume for two weeks, add supination work |
| Hips rise in a tuck planche | no pelvic tilt | go back to the lean; fix the shape with less load |
| Shoulders won't stay protracted | weak serratus | scapular push-ups, 3×12, every session |
| Wrist pain | extreme wrist extension | use parallettes — this is the main reason they exist |
| Tuck planche impossible after a good frog stand | expected — a big jump in d | spend longer on the lean, add advanced frog |

## Programming it

Twice a week. **Never more, in the first year.** 4–6 sets of a hold you can keep
perfect, ending every set while the shape is still good.

The volume rule that keeps people healthy: **total straight-arm time under
tension across the whole week should climb by no more than about 10%.** That
includes lever work in module 3 — it is the same tissue, and it does not care
which exercise caused the load.

If your elbows feel anything other than neutral, take the week off straight-arm
work entirely. You will lose almost nothing. Tendinopathy will cost you months.

## Target

**30 s at a lean where your shoulders are clearly past your wrists, with a flat
back and no elbow discomfort.** Then, and only then, tuck planche.
