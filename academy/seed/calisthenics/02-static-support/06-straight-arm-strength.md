This lesson has no new skill in it. It is about the tissue that every static in
this course loads, why it is the thing that actually limits you, and how to train
it without ending up injured — which is the single most common way people lose a
year of progress.

## Bent arm and straight arm are different sports

A pull-up and a front lever both "work your back". They do not load you in
remotely the same way.

**Bent-arm strength** is muscle shortening under load. The elbow is a hinge doing
work, the biceps and lats contract through range, and the tissue that adapts is
mostly muscle — which responds in **weeks**.

**Straight-arm strength** is a locked elbow transmitting force while the shoulder
resists rotation. Very little happens at the elbow *joint*, but an enormous amount
happens at the **tendons crossing it** — particularly the biceps tendon, which
crosses both the elbow and the shoulder and is loaded at length in every lever and
every planche.

Tendon adapts on a timescale of **months**. Collagen turnover is slow, and the
adaptation is largely about stiffness and cross-linking rather than size.

That mismatch is the whole problem: your muscles get strong enough for the next
rung long before your tendons do. You feel ready. You are not.

## What this means in practice

**Straight-arm work needs its own volume budget.** Add up all of it across the
week — planche lean, tuck planche, front lever, back lever, skin the cat,
support holds — as total seconds under tension. That single number is what your
connective tissue experiences. It does not care which exercise produced it.

**Increase that number by about 10% a week. Not more.** If last week was 400
seconds, this week is 440, not 600.

**Progress seconds before you progress leverage.** Going from 8 s to 12 s in a
tuck front lever is a smaller jump than going from tuck to advanced tuck. Take
the small step first, every time.

**Two straight-arm sessions a week beats four.** Tendon needs longer between
exposures than muscle does. Frequency that works beautifully for push-ups will
hurt you here.

## The warning signs, in the order they appear

1. A dull ache in the inner elbow **the day after** straight-arm work.
2. Stiffness in the elbow first thing in the morning that eases as you move.
3. Pain that arrives **during** the warm-up rather than during the work.
4. Pain that persists at rest.

**Stage 1 means reduce volume now.** Not next week. Halve your straight-arm
seconds for two weeks and the problem usually disappears. Stage 3 or 4 means stop
straight-arm work entirely and get it assessed — at that point you are months from
training normally, and pushing on makes it longer.

## Training tendon on purpose

Tendon responds to **slow, heavy, sustained** load far better than to fast or
explosive load:

- **Long holds** at a rung you own comfortably — 20–30 s, not 5 s of maximum
  effort. Time under tension is the stimulus.
- **Slow eccentrics** — 5–8 seconds of lowering through a position, which loads
  the tissue heavily with less joint stress than a maximal hold.
- **Isometrics at multiple angles** — hold the position at a few points through
  the range, not only the hardest one.

Notice what is missing: explosive straight-arm work. Kipping into a lever,
dropping suddenly into a german hang, bouncing at the bottom of a skin the cat.
Those load tendon fast and unpredictably, which is exactly what it tolerates
worst.

## The supporting work

Two small things, done consistently, prevent most straight-arm elbow problems:

**Slow supination and pronation with a light weight** — 2×15 each way, twice a
week. Loads the biceps tendon in its rotational role, gently.

**Wrist curls and reverse wrist curls** — 2×15 each, twice a week. The forearm
muscles cross the elbow, and a strong forearm changes what the elbow experiences.

Neither is exciting. Both take four minutes. They are the cheapest insurance in
this course.

## A sane weekly straight-arm budget

| Experience | Sessions/week | Total seconds under tension/week |
|---|---|---|
| First 3 months | 2 | 200–350 s |
| 3–12 months | 2 | 350–600 s |
| Beyond a year | 2–3 | 600–900 s |

Those are guidelines, not laws — but if you are well above the band for your
experience and your elbows are talking to you, you have found your answer.
