# Calisthenics: Zero to Expert

_From your first push-up to the planche — static and dynamic bodyweight strength, built as progressions._

This is the complete written companion to the course: thirty-two lessons in six
modules, organised as two parallel tracks — **static** strength (holding your
bodyweight still) and **dynamic** strength (moving it through range) — wrapped in
foundations at the front and programming at the back.

It is written, not filmed, and that is deliberate. A video shows you one rep from
one angle at one moment. What you actually need is the *reason* a position is
hard, the ladder that gets you there, and the number that tells you when to move
on. Those are things you read, mark up, and come back to.

---

<!-- FILE: 01-foundations/01-how-this-works.md -->
Welcome. This course turns "I train sometimes" into a deliberate practice that
takes you from the basics to expert-level bodyweight skills — and, more
importantly, teaches you the reasoning, so you can coach yourself when the plan
inevitably meets real life.

## The two tracks

Every calisthenics skill is one of two things:

- **Static** — holding your bodyweight still against gravity: the support hold,
  the L-sit, the handstand, the german hang, the back lever, the front lever,
  the planche.
- **Dynamic** — moving your bodyweight through a range: the push-up, the dip,
  the pull-up, the muscle-up, explosive work.

They build different qualities. Statics develop **straight-arm strength** and
tendon stiffness — connective tissue adapts slowly, and it is the usual reason a
lever plateaus. Dynamics develop **contractile strength through range**, which is
what actually moves you. Train them in parallel and each supports the other.
Train only one and you become the person who can do twenty pull-ups and cannot
hold a tuck front lever for five seconds — or the reverse.

So the course runs both at once. At any moment you will have **one static focus
and one dynamic focus**, and no more.

## How every lesson is built

The shape never changes, so you always know where to look:

1. **What it is** — the skill, and what it is actually for.
2. **The physics** — why it is hard, in numbers. This is the part most courses
   skip, and it is the part that lets you diagnose your own sticking point.
3. **The progression** — an ordered ladder. You train the rung you are on, plus
   a taste of the next one. Never more.
4. **Form cues** — the two or three that matter. Not fifteen.
5. **What goes wrong** — the specific failure modes, and the specific fix.
6. **Programming it** — sets, reps or seconds, frequency, rest.
7. **The target** — a number that says "move on."
8. **Self-check** — tick it before you progress.

## The five rules of progression

**One rung at a time.** Chasing the end skill is how people spend three years not
getting it. The ladder exists because each rung prepares tissue for the next.

**Quality before quantity.** One clean rep with a two-second pause is worth more
than five that use momentum. Momentum is the enemy of adaptation: it lets the
movement happen without the tissue being loaded.

**One variable per week.** Reps, seconds, leverage, or load — pick one and change
it. If you change three at once and something starts to hurt, you have learned
nothing about which one did it.

**Log everything.** "Felt hard" is not data. "3×5 negatives, 4 s each, last set
broke down" is data. You cannot apply progressive overload to a feeling.

**Deload before you need to.** Connective tissue recovers more slowly than
muscle and gives you very little warning. Module 6 covers this properly.

## What you need

A **pull-up bar**, a **floor**, and ideally **parallettes** (or two low, stable
surfaces of equal height) and a **resistance band**. That is the entire kit.

Rings are a wonderful addition and not a requirement. Where they change things,
the course says so — mostly they make everything harder by removing stability,
which is useful later and counterproductive early.

## How long this actually takes

Honestly: the first pull-up takes most people 6–12 weeks from a dead hang. A
clean muscle-up takes most people 9–18 months from their first pull-up. A full
front lever takes years, and plenty of strong people never get one.

The course is built so that every one of those stages is *itself* worth training,
rather than a waiting room for the next thing.

Next: the physics. Twenty minutes here will save you months of guessing.

<!-- FILE: 01-foundations/02-the-physics.md -->
You do not need to enjoy physics to train. You do need four ideas, because every
plateau you will ever hit is one of them — and knowing which one turns "I'm
stuck" into "I know exactly what to change."

## 1. Torque is the only thing your shoulders feel

When you hold a static position, what your body resists is not your weight. It is
**torque** — the twisting effect of your weight about the joint holding you up.

![The lever-arm equation drawn on a hanging body](/academy/figures/lever-arm.svg)

```
τ = m · g · d
```

- **m** is your mass. You cannot change it quickly, and you should not try to.
- **g** is gravity. Not negotiable.
- **d** is the horizontal distance from the pivot to your **centre of mass**.

Two of the three are fixed. **d is the entire game.** Every progression in the
static half of this course is a way of shrinking d so you can train a position
you cannot yet hold, then letting it grow back as you get stronger.

This is why "just try the front lever" is useless advice and "tuck front lever,
8 seconds" is good advice. They are the same skill at two values of d.

## 2. Your centre of mass moves when you move

Your centre of mass is the mass-weighted average position of every part of you.
Standing upright it sits roughly behind your navel. Tuck your knees and it
travels toward your chest. Reach overhead and it rises.

That is the mechanism behind every tuck, straddle and half-lay in this course.
You are not doing an easier *version* of the skill. You are doing the same skill
with your centre of mass relocated.

Here is what that is worth, for the front lever:

![The four front-lever positions with their measured lever arms](/academy/figures/front-lever-ladder.svg)

Those percentages are computed, not estimated. The body model uses standard
segment masses and measures where the centre of mass actually lands in each
position.

Now the part that catches people out:

![Where front-lever torque comes from, by body segment](/academy/figures/torque-breakdown.svg)

**Your torso alone is about a third of the total, and no amount of tucking
removes any of it.** A tuck can only fold away the legs. That is why even a deep
tuck still asks for roughly two-thirds of the full hold, and why a tuck front
lever is not a beginner move.

## 3. Difficulty does not scale with torque

If a tuck front lever is 65% of the torque of a full one, why does it feel
enormously easier — far more than 35% easier?

Because you are working near the top of your strength curve, and up there the
relationship between load and endurance is brutally steep. Drop the demand by a
third when you are at 95% of maximum and you might go from a 2-second hold to a
20-second one. The *torque* fell by a third. The *time you can hold it* went up
tenfold.

Two consequences, both of which shape how this course is written:

- **Small reductions in d buy large amounts of training volume.** That is why
  these ladders have so many rungs. Each is a modest change in leverage and a
  large change in how much quality work you can accumulate.
- **Small increases in d cost you just as steeply.** When you extend one leg out
  of a tuck and a 20-second hold collapses to 4, nothing has gone wrong. That is
  the curve behaving normally. Do not read it as failure.

## 4. Muscles are stronger at lowering than at lifting

A muscle produces more force while lengthening under load (**eccentric**) than
while holding (**isometric**), and more holding than while shortening
(**concentric**). Roughly:

![Relative force capacity: eccentric, isometric, concentric](/academy/figures/force-capacity.svg)

That gap is the most useful fact in this course, because it means:

**You can train a movement you cannot yet perform, by doing the lowering half of
it.**

Cannot do a pull-up? You can almost certainly lower yourself from the top slowly.
Cannot hold an advanced tuck? You can lower through it. Every "I can't do this
yet" in the syllabus has an eccentric answer, and that answer is usually the
fastest route to the concentric version.

It is also why eccentrics leave you sorer and need more recovery: more force
through the tissue, more micro-damage, more adaptation, more time.

## Putting the four together — a worked example

You want a front lever. You can hold a tuck for 15 seconds and an advanced tuck
for 3.

1. **Torque.** The advanced tuck is 76% of full, the tuck 65%. You are stuck on an
   11-percentage-point step.
2. **Centre of mass.** You do not have to take the whole step. Extend *one* leg
   halfway and you land between the rungs.
3. **The difficulty curve.** At 3 seconds you are far too close to your limit to
   accumulate useful volume. Back off to something you hold for 8–12.
4. **Eccentrics.** Add slow lowers *through* the advanced tuck to load the
   position you cannot yet hold.

That is the whole method. Everything after this is the method applied to specific
skills.

<!-- FILE: 01-foundations/03-the-assessment.md -->
Before you train anything you need to know where you actually are — not where you
were at your fittest, and not where you would like to be. Everything that follows
is calibrated off these numbers.

Do the whole assessment in one session, rested. Write every number down. It takes
about forty minutes.

## Before you start

Warm up properly: five minutes of easy movement, then the joint prep from the
next lesson. An assessment done cold measures your warm-up, not your strength.

Stop any test the moment form breaks. A rep with a bent arm in a straight-arm
test is not a rep, and counting it means training the wrong rung for weeks.

## The seven tests

### 1. Dead hang — grip and shoulder tolerance

Hang from the bar, arms straight, feet clear of the floor. Time it to the first
moment your grip genuinely slips.

| Result | What it means |
|---|---|
| under 15 s | grip is your limiter before anything else is |
| 15–30 s | normal untrained starting point |
| 30–60 s | ready for everything in module 3 |
| over 60 s | grip will not limit you for a long time |

### 2. Push-up — horizontal push

Maximum consecutive reps. Chest to fist-height off the floor, body in one line,
no sagging hips, full lockout at the top. Stop at the first rep that breaks form.

| Result | Start at |
|---|---|
| 0 | incline push-ups |
| 1–5 | knee push-ups for volume, full push-ups for quality |
| 6–15 | full push-ups, begin the progression |
| 16+ | move toward dips and decline variants |

### 3. Pull-up — vertical pull

Maximum consecutive reps from a full dead hang, chin clearly over the bar, no
kipping and no leg drive.

| Result | Start at |
|---|---|
| 0 | negatives and band-assisted work |
| 1–4 | greasing the groove, and the first-rep protocol |
| 5–10 | volume work, then archer and weighted |
| 11+ | weighted pull-ups and muscle-up preparation |

### 4. Hollow hold — trunk

Lie on your back, lower back pressed flat into the floor, shoulders and legs
lifted. Time it to the moment your lower back lifts off — **not** to the moment
you get tired. The back leaving the floor is the end of the test.

| Result | What it means |
|---|---|
| under 15 s | this is your priority, before any lever work |
| 15–45 s | normal; train it alongside everything else |
| over 45 s | your trunk will not be the limiter |

### 5. Support hold — straight-arm push

On parallettes, dip bars, or two stable surfaces: arms locked, shoulders pushed
down away from your ears, body vertical. Time to the first shrug or bent elbow.

| Result | Start at |
|---|---|
| under 10 s | build the support hold itself first |
| 10–30 s | ready for the L-sit ladder |
| over 30 s | ready for planche lean |

### 6. Shoulder extension — the safety test

Stand with your hands behind you on a low bar or bench, and slowly let your
weight sink until you feel a firm stretch across the front of the shoulder. Do
not push into pain. Note roughly how far your shoulders travel behind your torso.

This is not a strength test. It gates module 3 entirely: **if your shoulders
cannot get into extension comfortably, do not train skin the cat or back
levers.** That module explains exactly why.

### 7. Wrist tolerance

Kneel, hands flat on the floor, fingers forward, and lean your weight over them.
Repeat with fingers pointing back toward your knees. Note any pain or pinching in
either direction.

Wrists gate the entire handstand and planche line. Nothing in this course injures
more people than untrained wrists meeting a lot of load.

## Turning seven numbers into a starting point

Take your **lowest** relevant score, not your best. A chain trains at the speed of
its weakest link, and the weakest link is where injuries happen.

Then pick exactly two things:

- **one static focus** — support hold, L-sit, german hang, or a tuck lever
- **one dynamic focus** — push-up, dip, pull-up, or muscle-up preparation

Write them at the top of your log. They stay there for at least four weeks.

## Re-testing

Re-test all seven **every six weeks**, in the same order, rested, warmed up the
same way. Not more often — shorter intervals measure how you slept, not how you
adapted.

## Self-check

- [ ] All seven numbers written down, with today's date
- [ ] I picked my starting rung from my lowest score, not my highest
- [ ] One static focus and one dynamic focus, and only one of each
- [ ] Next re-test date is in my calendar
<!-- FILE: 01-foundations/04-joint-prep-and-mobility.md -->
Calisthenics loads joints in positions most adult bodies have not visited in
twenty years. Wrists get compressed under full bodyweight, shoulders get pulled
into deep extension, elbows get loaded straight and locked. None of those are
dangerous. All of them are dangerous *cold*, or *suddenly*.

This lesson is the ten minutes that goes before every session for the rest of
your training life. It is not a stretching routine and it is not optional.

## Why tissue needs a different kind of warm-up

Muscle warms up fast — a few minutes of movement and blood flow does most of it.
**Tendon and ligament do not.** Connective tissue is far less vascular, stiffens
when cold, and adapts on a timescale of months rather than weeks.

This produces the most common injury pattern in calisthenics: your muscles get
strong enough for the next rung long before your tendons do, you feel ready, you
take the step, and an elbow or a wrist complains three weeks later. Golfer's
elbow from straight-arm work is practically a rite of passage, and it is almost
always this.

The prep below does two jobs: it raises tissue temperature, and it takes each
joint through the range you are about to load it in, under a little control, so
the first thing that surprises your elbow is not your bodyweight.

## The routine — about 10 minutes

### Wrists — 3 minutes

The single highest-return three minutes in the course. Do all of these on the
floor, kneeling, moving slowly and never into sharp pain.

1. **Fingers forward, rock forward and back** — 10 slow rocks. Palms flat,
   weight shifting over the hands.
2. **Fingers backward, rock back and forth** — 10 slow rocks. This one will feel
   awful at first and improves quickly.
3. **Fingers sideways, both directions** — 8 each way.
4. **Palm-up rocks** — backs of the hands on the floor, fingers pointing back.
   8 gentle rocks. Stop early if it pinches.
5. **Fist rocks** — on knuckles, 10 rocks. Teaches the wrist to work in a
   neutral, stacked position.
6. **Finger pulses** — palms flat, lift the palm and press through the
   fingertips, 15 reps. This is the exact motion that saves a handstand.

### Shoulders — 4 minutes

1. **Band or broomstick dislocates** — 10 slow reps, hands wide enough that
   nothing pinches, then narrow the grip a little each week.
2. **Scapular push-ups** — in a plank, protract and retract the shoulder blades
   without bending the elbows. 12 reps. Teaches the scapula to move
   independently of the arm, which is most of module 2.
3. **Scapular pulls** — hang from the bar with straight arms, pull your shoulders
   down away from your ears, hold 2 s, release. 8 reps. This is the foundation of
   every pulling skill, and it appears again in module 5.
4. **Wall slides** — back to the wall, arms in a goalpost, slide up and down
   keeping wrists and elbows in contact. 10 reps.

### Elbows — 2 minutes

1. **Straight-arm circles** — arms locked out, small circles, 10 each direction.
2. **Supination/pronation with a light weight** — hold something light, rotate
   palm up and palm down slowly, 10 each side. The most effective single thing
   you can do to keep golfer's elbow away.

### Hips and spine — 1 minute

1. **Cat-cow** — 8 slow reps, breathing with the movement.
2. **Deep squat hold** — 30 s, heels down if you can.

## Rules

**Never stretch hard before training.** A long static stretch before a session
reduces your ability to produce force for a while afterward. Save real
flexibility work for after, or for its own session.

**Some of this should feel unpleasant. None of it should feel sharp.** Dull
stretch and a strong pull are fine. Pinching, tingling, electrical or radiating
sensations are a stop signal — that is nerve, not tissue, and it does not respond
to "pushing through."

**The prep scales with the session.** Handstand or planche day means more wrists.
Lever day means more shoulders. Pull day means more scapular work.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Likely cause | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Wrist pain on the heel of the hand | too much load, too fast, extended wrist | reduce volume, add fist and parallette work, rebuild the rock-throughs |
| Inner-elbow ache after lever work | straight-arm load outpacing tendon | drop one rung, halve the straight-arm volume for two weeks, add supination work |
| Front-of-shoulder pinch overhead | poor overhead range, or a rushed handstand | wall slides and dislocates daily, and stay off free handstands until it clears |
| Anything that tingles or radiates | nerve, not muscle | stop that movement entirely and get it looked at |

## Programming it

Every session, before anything else. Ten minutes. It does not vary and it does
not get skipped because you are short on time — if you are short on time, cut the
workout, not the prep.

Once or twice a week, add a **separate** ten minutes of slow loaded stretching
(deep squat, german hang, pancake) on a rest day or well after training.

## Self-check

- [ ] I can kneel with fingers pointing backward and rock without sharp pain
- [ ] I can do 10 band dislocates without shrugging or pinching
- [ ] I can move my shoulder blades without bending my elbows
- [ ] The prep happens before *every* session, including short ones

<!-- FILE: 01-foundations/05-hollow-body-and-bracing.md -->
Two shapes underpin everything in this course. Learn them now and every skill
gets easier; skip them and you will spend years wondering why your levers sag and
your handstand bananas.

![Hollow body compared with an arch](/academy/figures/hollow-arch.svg)

## What a brace actually does

Picture your body in a front lever as a beam supported at one end — your hands.
Gravity pulls down along its whole length. A beam that cannot resist bending
**folds in the middle**, and in a human that fold happens at the hips.

The brace is what turns a floppy chain of segments into a single rigid beam. When
you cannot hold a position "because my core is weak", what is usually happening
is not weakness — it is that the segments are not connected, so the force never
reaches the muscles that could hold it.

This is why bracing pays off out of all proportion to the effort. It does not add
strength. It stops you leaking the strength you already have.

## The hollow position

Lie on your back:

1. Press your **lower back flat into the floor**. Not nearly flat. Flat.
2. **Ribs down** — do not let the bottom of your ribcage flare toward the ceiling.
3. **Pelvis tucked** slightly, as if starting a sit-up without moving.
4. Now lift shoulders and legs a few centimetres, keeping the back flat.

The moment the lower back lifts off the floor, the position is over. That moment
is the honest measure of the exercise, not how tired you feel.

## Why the arch matters too

The opposite shape — the superman — trains the back of the same chain. You need
both: hollow for every lever and handstand, arch for shoulder health and for the
back extensors that stop the hollow becoming your only posture.

A body that can only hollow is as unbalanced as one that can only arch.

## The progression

| Rung | What to do | Target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Dead bug** — on your back, opposite arm and leg extend slowly, back stays flat | 10 slow reps each side |
| 2 | **Tuck hollow hold** — knees and arms tucked, back flat | 30 s |
| 3 | **One-leg hollow** — one leg extended, one tucked | 30 s each side |
| 4 | **Hollow hold, arms tucked** — legs straight, arms on chest | 30 s |
| 5 | **Full hollow hold** — legs straight, arms overhead | 45 s |
| 6 | **Hollow rocks** — full hollow, rocking from shoulders to hips | 30 s of smooth rocking |

The tell for every rung is identical: **the moment the lower back leaves the
floor, the set is finished.** Not when it gets hard. When the shape breaks.

## The arch progression

| Rung | What to do | Target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Prone arm and leg lift** — face down, lift opposite arm and leg | 10 each side |
| 2 | **Superman hold** — arms and legs lifted, hold | 30 s |
| 3 | **Superman rocks** — rock forward and back | 30 s |

## Form cues that carry into every other lesson

**"Ribs down."** The single most useful cue in calisthenics. It appears in the
handstand, the planche lean, the front lever and the L-sit, and it means the
same thing every time.

**"Squeeze your glutes."** Locks the pelvis to the ribcage. Without it, the hips
are a hinge, and a hinge in a lever is a leak.

**"Point your toes."** Not for aesthetics. Pointing the toes tensions the whole
posterior chain down to the ankle and helps the leg behave as one rigid segment
rather than two.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Lower back lifts immediately | position too hard | drop a rung; a shorter lever is not cheating |
| Neck hurts in a hollow | pulling the head with the neck | look at your knees, keep a fist of space under the chin, let the shoulders do it |
| Hips sag in a lever | brace, not strength | go back to rung 4 and hold it properly |
| Fine on the floor, sags on the bar | you have never braced under load | add tuck hangs and hanging knee raises |

## Programming it

Three to five times a week, **before** your main work rather than as a finisher.
Bracing is a skill, and skills are trained fresh. Two or three sets of one rung,
plus one set of the arch version.

It should never be the hardest thing you do that day. It should be the most
precise.

## Self-check

- [ ] 45 s full hollow with the lower back never leaving the floor
- [ ] 30 s superman hold
- [ ] "Ribs down", "squeeze glutes", "point toes" happen without being reminded
- [ ] I can keep the shape while hanging from a bar

<!-- FILE: 02-static-support/01-support-hold.md -->
The support hold is the first straight-arm position in the course, and the
gateway to the entire pushing half of the static track. Everything above it — the
L-sit, the planche lean, the handstand, the dip — assumes you own it.

It looks like nothing: you hold yourself up on two bars, arms locked. It is not
nothing. It is the position where your shoulders learn to be **active** under
load, and where most people discover that they have been hanging off their joints
rather than holding themselves with muscle.

## What it is

On parallettes, dip bars, or two stable surfaces of equal height:

- arms **locked straight**
- shoulders **pushed down** hard, away from the ears
- body **vertical**, hollow, ribs down, glutes tight
- legs together, toes pointed
- eyes forward, not down

## The physics

Your arms are struts. A locked elbow transmits force through bone with almost no
muscular cost — which is why you can hold a support far longer than you can hold
a bent-arm position at the same height.

The work is at the **shoulder**, and specifically it is the job of keeping the
shoulder girdle *pushed down*. Let that go and you sink between your shoulder
blades: the joint takes load passively, the muscles that should be working switch
off, and you are now resting on connective tissue. It is comfortable. It is also
exactly how people accumulate shoulder problems.

The cue "push the floor away" is not poetry. It is the difference between an
active support and a hang.

## The progression

| Rung | What to do | Target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Feet-assisted support** — bars low, feet on the floor taking some weight | 30 s |
| 2 | **Support hold, knees tucked** | 20 s |
| 3 | **Full support hold** | 30 s |
| 4 | **Support hold with shrugs** — sink and press up, scapula only, elbows locked | 10 reps |
| 5 | **Ring support hold** — rings turned out at the bottom | 20 s |
| 6 | **Weighted support** — light vest or belt | 20 s |

Rung 5 deserves a warning. Rings remove stability, so the shoulder has to control
rotation as well as height, and the jump in difficulty from bars is much larger
than it looks. Do not attempt it before a clean 30 s on bars.

## Form cues

**Push down, not up.** You are not shrugging your body upward; you are pressing
the bars down and away.

**Ribs down, glutes tight.** Without it you will hang in your lower back, and the
"support hold" becomes a passive slump with straight arms.

**Elbows locked and rotated.** Aim the inside of your elbow forward. It stacks
the joint and cues the whole arm to stay straight.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Shoulders creep up to the ears | not pressing down | go back to rung 1; do shrug reps to feel the range |
| Elbows unlock under fatigue | triceps or a sagging brace | shorten the hold; stop the set at the first bend |
| Wrist pain | bars too wide, or an over-extended wrist | narrow the grip, use parallettes, do the wrist prep |
| Body swings forward | hips not braced | tuck the pelvis, squeeze the glutes |
| Rings wobble uncontrollably | normal | you are not ready for rings — go back to bars |

## Programming it

Three times a week. Accumulate **60–90 seconds of total hold time** per session,
in sets that end while the shape is still clean — so five sets of 15 s, not one
heroic set of 40 and three ugly ones.

Total hold time is the number to progress. Add about 10% a week.

## Target

**30 seconds, motionless, shoulders down, body vertical.** Then move to the L-sit
ladder.

## Self-check

- [ ] 30 s support hold with no shrug and no elbow bend
- [ ] 10 clean scapular shrugs in support
- [ ] No wrist pain during or after
- [ ] I can breathe normally in the hold — if you cannot, you are bracing too hard
<!-- FILE: 02-static-support/02-l-sit-progression.md -->
The L-sit is where the lever-arm idea stops being theory. It is one position with
a dial on it, and you turn the dial by straightening your knees.

## What it is

Supported on your hands — parallettes, dip bars, or the floor — with your legs
held straight out in front of you, parallel to the ground, making an L.

## The physics

![Torque about the hands in an L-sit](/academy/figures/l-sit.svg)

Your hands are the pivot. Your legs and torso hang off it, and the torque about
your shoulders is `m · g · d`, where d is the horizontal distance from your hands
to your centre of mass.

Tuck your knees and the mass of your legs sits close to the pivot. Extend them
and it travels away. Nothing about you has changed — not your strength, not your
weight. **The lever changed.**

So the ladder below is not four different exercises. It is one exercise at four
values of d, and knowing that tells you what to do when you get stuck: you do not
need a different movement, you need an intermediate d. Extending one leg halfway,
or bending one knee slightly, is always available to you.

The second demand is **compression** — actively holding the legs up, which is hip
flexor work, not just core work. Many people have the shoulder strength for an
L-sit and cannot get their legs level. That is a compression problem and it has
its own fix, below.

## The progression

| Rung | What to do | Target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Support hold** — prerequisite | 30 s |
| 2 | **Tuck L-sit** — knees to chest | 20 s |
| 3 | **Advanced tuck** — thighs parallel to floor, shins hanging | 20 s |
| 4 | **One-leg L-sit** — one leg straight, one tucked | 15 s each side |
| 5 | **Full L-sit** on parallettes | 15 s |
| 6 | **Full L-sit on the floor** — much harder, needs long arms or high compression | 10 s |
| 7 | **V-sit** — legs above parallel | 5 s |

Rung 6 surprises people. On the floor your shoulders are lower, so your legs must
clear the ground with nowhere to hang — the required compression jumps sharply.
It is normal to hold a solid L-sit on parallettes and fail completely on the
floor.

## The compression fix

If your shoulders are fine but your legs will not come up, train compression
directly, on a rest day or after your main work:

- **Seated compression lifts** — sit with legs straight, hands on the floor, lift
  the heels off the ground and hold 5 s. 5 reps.
- **Pike compression** — seated, pull your chest toward your thighs with your
  hands, then let go and hold the position with muscle for 5 s. 5 reps.
- **Hanging leg raises** — straight legs, slow, no swing. 3×8.

## Form cues

**Push the floor away.** Shoulders down, chest tall. A sunk shoulder shortens
nothing and costs everything.

**Knees locked, toes pointed.** A soft knee is a shorter lever, which means you
are training a rung below the one you think you are on.

**Lean back very slightly.** Not a slump — a small shift of the shoulders behind
the hands makes the legs come up. Too much and it becomes a different exercise.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Legs below parallel | compression, not strength | do the compression work above; hold the rung you own |
| Shoulders shrug up | losing the support position | go back to the support hold |
| Knees bend as fatigue arrives | at the edge of your rung | end sets earlier; quality over duration |
| Lower back rounds hard | trying to cheat compression by curling | tuck the pelvis deliberately, drop a rung |
| Can do it on bars, not the floor | expected | keep both in the plan, they progress separately |

## Programming it

Three times a week. **45–60 s total hold time** per session in clean sets. Add
about 10% per week to total time before you move a rung.

Pair it with compression work twice a week, never on the same day as heavy
lever work — the hip flexors are already busy there.

## Target

**15 seconds, full L-sit on parallettes, legs at or above parallel, knees
locked.** Then move to planche lean.

## Self-check

- [ ] 15 s full L-sit, legs level or higher
- [ ] Knees stay locked for the whole hold
- [ ] Shoulders stay pushed down
- [ ] I can hold an advanced tuck for 20 s as my back-off set

<!-- FILE: 02-static-support/03-tuck-planche-and-lean.md -->
The planche is the hardest position in this course and the one most likely to
injure you if approached impatiently. It is also the purest demonstration of the
lever-arm rule, because the planche has a continuous dial rather than discrete
rungs: **lean**.

## What it is

Holding your body horizontal, face down, supported only on your hands, with
nothing touching the ground. The planche lean is the entry: hands on the floor,
feet still down, shoulders leaning forward past the hands.

## The physics

![Planche lean and the growing lever arm](/academy/figures/planche-lean.svg)

In a planche, your hands are the pivot and your centre of mass is **behind** them,
so your shoulders must produce a forward torque to stop you rotating backward.
The further you lean, the further your centre of mass travels past the hands, and
the larger d becomes.

That is what makes the planche special: **d is continuous**. You do not jump from
one rung to the next, you add a few degrees. This is enormously useful, because
it means you can always find a load you can hold — and dangerous, because it
means you can always find a load you cannot.

A planche also asks something the other statics do not: the load is on
**straight-arm shoulder flexion with the biceps tendon under tension at the
elbow**. That combination is exactly the one that produces biceps tendinopathy
and golfer's elbow when volume climbs too fast. The rules below are not caution
for its own sake.

## The progression

| Rung | What to do | Target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Plank** — perfect hollow, shoulders over hands | 60 s |
| 2 | **Planche lean, small** — shoulders just past the wrists | 30 s |
| 3 | **Planche lean, moderate** — shoulders clearly past, weight forward | 20 s |
| 4 | **Frog stand** — knees resting on the elbows, feet off | 30 s |
| 5 | **Tuck planche** — knees off the elbows, hips at shoulder height | 15 s |
| 6 | **Advanced tuck planche** — back flat, thighs vertical-ish | 10 s |
| 7 | **Straddle planche** | 5 s |

Most people should spend **months** on rungs 2 and 3. That is not a failure of
the plan, it is the plan. Straight-arm tissue adapts slowly, and the lean is
where it adapts safely.

## Measuring the lean

"Lean more" is not a number, so make it one. Set up in a plank with your hands on
a line, mark where your shoulders sit, and measure the horizontal distance from
wrist to shoulder. Write it down in centimetres.

Now "add lean" becomes "add one centimetre this week," which is a real, repeatable
progression instead of a vague instruction.

## Form cues

**Protract hard.** Push the floor away and let your upper back round — the
opposite of most gym advice, and correct here. A retracted scapula in a planche
puts the load onto the joint instead of the muscle.

**Posterior pelvic tilt.** Tuck the pelvis under. Without it the hips rise, the
lower back takes the strain, and the position becomes a different, easier,
uglier one.

**Elbows locked, inner elbow forward.** Non-negotiable. A bent-arm planche is a
different skill with a different injury profile.

**Fingers gripping the floor.** They are the brakes; they stop you tipping forward
and they teach the hand to participate.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Inner elbow pain | classic straight-arm overuse | stop, drop lean, halve the volume for two weeks, add supination work |
| Hips rise in a tuck planche | no pelvic tilt | go back to the lean; fix the shape with less load |
| Shoulders won't stay protracted | weak serratus | scapular push-ups, 3×12, every session |
| Wrist pain | extreme wrist extension | use parallettes — this is the main reason they exist |
| Tuck planche impossible after a good frog stand | expected — a big jump in d | spend longer on the lean, add advanced frog |

## Programming it

Twice a week. **Never more, in the first year.** 4–6 sets of a hold you can keep
perfect, ending every set while the shape is still good.

The volume rule that keeps people healthy: **total straight-arm time under
tension across the whole week should climb by no more than about 10%.** That
includes lever work in module 3 — it is the same tissue, and it does not care
which exercise caused the load.

If your elbows feel anything other than neutral, take the week off straight-arm
work entirely. You will lose almost nothing. Tendinopathy will cost you months.

## Target

**30 s at a lean where your shoulders are clearly past your wrists, with a flat
back and no elbow discomfort.** Then, and only then, tuck planche.

## Self-check

- [ ] 30 s moderate planche lean, measured and logged in centimetres
- [ ] Scapulae stay protracted the whole time
- [ ] Pelvis stays tucked, hips do not rise
- [ ] Elbows are completely quiet the day after

<!-- FILE: 02-static-support/04-handstand-wall.md -->
The handstand is the only skill in this course that is mostly **not** a strength
problem. If you can hold a 30-second support hold you already have the strength
to hold a handstand. What you do not have is the balance, and balance is a
learned control problem that takes hundreds of short exposures.

Which is why the handstand gets two lessons, and why the programming looks
nothing like the rest of the course.

## The physics

![Centre of mass over the base of support in a handstand](/academy/figures/handstand-balance.svg)

One rule: **your centre of mass must stay over your base of support.** The base
is the patch of floor under your hands — about the size of two spread hands, and
much longer front-to-back than side-to-side.

When your centre of mass drifts past the front edge (your fingertips), you fall
over. When it drifts past the back edge (the heel of the hand), you fall back.
A handstand is not a position you hold. It is a **continuous correction** you run
several times a second.

Your corrections come from three places, in order of how often you should use
them:

1. **Fingers** — press the fingertips into the floor to stop going over. This is
   90% of balance, and it is why the finger pulses in your joint prep matter.
2. **Heel of the hand** — press down to stop falling back.
3. **Everything else** — hips, shoulders, legs. These are emergency measures. If
   you are correcting with your hips, your hands stopped doing their job.

## Why the wall first

A free handstand has two problems at once: staying up, and not falling over.
The wall removes the second so you can learn the first.

There are two wall positions, and they are not interchangeable:

- **Belly-to-wall (chest facing the wall)** — the good one. Your body is in the
  correct straight line and you can walk your feet in until you are nearly
  vertical. Use this one.
- **Back-to-wall (back facing the wall)** — the common one. It encourages an
  arched banana shape, because the wall lets your hips sag. Use it only for
  getting comfortable being upside down in the first week or two.

## The progression

| Rung | What to do | Target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Wrist prep** — from module 1 | done every session |
| 2 | **Plank to pike** — walk feet toward hands, hips stacking over shoulders | 30 s pike hold |
| 3 | **Wall walk** — feet on wall, walk hands in toward it | hands within 20 cm of the wall |
| 4 | **Belly-to-wall hold** | 60 s total across sets |
| 5 | **Belly-to-wall, one foot off** | 15 s each side |
| 6 | **Belly-to-wall, both feet off briefly** | 3–5 s balance |
| 7 | **Heel pulls** — push off the wall, find balance, return | 10 attempts |

## The line

A handstand has one correct shape and three common wrong ones. The correct one:

wrists → elbows → shoulders → hips → knees → ankles, in a **straight vertical
line**, with ribs down, pelvis tucked, legs together and toes pointed.

The wrong ones:

- **The banana** — arched lower back, hips forward of the shoulders. By far the
  most common. It is a shape problem, not a strength problem, and the fix is
  belly-to-wall plus ribs down.
- **The pike** — hips behind the shoulders. Usually fear; you are hedging.
- **The bent arm** — elbows soft. Nearly always shoulder range, not triceps
  strength: if you cannot get your arms fully overhead standing up, you cannot do
  it upside down either.

## Programming it — this one is different

**Frequency beats volume.** Balance is a motor skill, and motor skills are built
by many short exposures, not by long tiring ones. Five or six days a week, ten to
fifteen minutes, is much better than two hour-long sessions.

Do handstand work **first**, while you are fresh. A tired handstand practice
teaches your nervous system a tired handstand.

A good fifteen-minute session:

1. wrist prep — 3 min
2. wall walks — 3 sets
3. belly-to-wall holds — 4 sets, stopping before the shape breaks
4. heel pulls — 10 attempts
5. wrist decompression (fists, gentle stretch) — 2 min

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Banana back | ribs flared, no pelvic tilt | belly-to-wall only; cue ribs down and squeeze glutes |
| Wrist pain after two sessions | too much too soon | halve the volume, more prep, use parallettes |
| Elbows bend | overhead shoulder range | dislocates and wall slides daily |
| Falling immediately every time | correcting with hips instead of fingers | slow down, use the wall, learn finger pressure first |
| Panic when upside down | normal | learn to bail (next lesson) — fear disappears once you can exit safely |

## Target

**60 seconds belly-to-wall in a straight line, and 5 seconds of unsupported
balance from heel pulls.** Then the free handstand.

## Self-check

- [ ] 60 s belly-to-wall, hands within 20 cm of the wall
- [ ] Ribs down and pelvis tucked without being reminded
- [ ] I correct with my fingers, not my hips
- [ ] No wrist pain the next day
<!-- FILE: 02-static-support/05-handstand-freestanding.md -->
Everything from the wall lesson still applies. What changes is that nothing
catches you, so before you spend a single session chasing balance, you learn to
fall.

## Learn to bail first

Fear is the largest single obstacle to a free handstand, and fear is rational
until you have an exit. Practise both of these on a soft surface until they are
automatic:

**The pirouette-out** — the one you will use 95% of the time. As you feel
yourself going over, turn one hand out, let the shoulders rotate, and step down
to the side. You end up standing, facing sideways. Drill it deliberately twenty
times before you need it.

**The forward roll-out** — for going over the front. Tuck the chin, bend the
arms, and roll along the upper back. Never put a straight arm down to catch
yourself: that is how wrists and elbows break.

Once you can bail without thinking, the handstand stops being frightening and
starts being interesting. Everyone improves faster after this.

## The kick-up

The most common mistake is kicking too hard. You are not launching yourself; you
are placing your hips over your hands and letting the legs follow.

1. **Lunge** — strong front leg, arms already locked overhead by your ears.
2. **Hands down about a shoulder-width apart**, fingers spread, a little before
   your front foot.
3. **Swing the back leg up**, push off the front foot gently.
4. **Stack the hips over the shoulders.** This is the actual goal, not the legs.
5. **Bring the legs together** at the top.

Aim to arrive *slightly short* of vertical and let your fingers pull you the rest
of the way. Kicking past vertical and relying on your shoulders to catch it is
the habit that makes handstands exhausting.

## Balance, in order of priority

1. **Fingers.** Going over the front → press the fingertips down. This should
   happen continuously, like a small constant conversation with the floor.
2. **Heel of the hand.** Falling back → press the heel.
3. **Shoulder shrug.** Push the floor away harder; it raises your centre of mass
   and slows everything down.
4. **Hip or leg adjustment.** Last resort. Splitting the legs is a real correction
   but it costs you the shape.

## The progression

| Rung | What to do | Target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Bails** — pirouette and roll-out | automatic, 20 reps each |
| 2 | **Kick-up to the wall, controlled** | lands softly, no bang |
| 3 | **Heel pulls** — off the wall, find balance | 5 s hold |
| 4 | **Free kick-up, bail every time** | 20 attempts, no fear |
| 5 | **Free handstand** | 10 s |
| 6 | **Free handstand** | 30 s |
| 7 | **Handstand shoulder taps** | 5 taps each side |

## Programming it

Same as the wall lesson, and it bears repeating because everybody ignores it:
**short and often**. Ten to fifteen minutes, five or six days a week, first thing
in the session.

Set a target of **attempts**, not time. "Thirty kick-ups" is a session you can
finish. "Hold a handstand for thirty seconds" is a session that ends in
frustration.

Track your **best hold each week**. It will go 3 s, 4 s, 3 s, 6 s, 4 s, 11 s. That
is what learning a balance skill looks like from the inside — it is not linear and
the bad days are not a sign of anything.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Always over-kicking | too much leg, not enough hip | practise kicking to a wall and stopping short |
| Only balance for 1–2 s | correcting too late | more reps, smaller corrections, use the fingers earlier |
| Shoulders tire in 10 s | not pushing tall | shrug harder; a sunk handstand is much harder work |
| Great at the wall, hopeless free | the wall was doing the balancing | more heel pulls, fewer static wall holds |
| Wrists hurt | volume | shorter sessions, more of them; parallettes for some sets |

## Target

**30 seconds of free handstand in a straight line.** That is a genuinely
respectable skill and most people who claim it cannot actually do it.

## Self-check

- [ ] I can bail without thinking, in both directions
- [ ] I kick up without slamming into the wall
- [ ] 10 s free handstand
- [ ] I correct with fingers first, hips last

<!-- FILE: 02-static-support/06-straight-arm-strength.md -->
This lesson has no new skill in it. It is about the tissue that every static in
this course loads, why it is the thing that actually limits you, and how to train
it without ending up injured — which is the single most common way people lose a
year of progress.

## Bent arm and straight arm are different sports

A pull-up and a front lever both "work your back". They do not load you in
remotely the same way.

**Bent-arm strength** is muscle shortening under load. The elbow is a hinge doing
work, the biceps and lats contract through range, and the tissue that adapts is
mostly muscle — which responds in **weeks**.

**Straight-arm strength** is a locked elbow transmitting force while the shoulder
resists rotation. Very little happens at the elbow *joint*, but an enormous amount
happens at the **tendons crossing it** — particularly the biceps tendon, which
crosses both the elbow and the shoulder and is loaded at length in every lever and
every planche.

Tendon adapts on a timescale of **months**. Collagen turnover is slow, and the
adaptation is largely about stiffness and cross-linking rather than size.

That mismatch is the whole problem: your muscles get strong enough for the next
rung long before your tendons do. You feel ready. You are not.

## What this means in practice

**Straight-arm work needs its own volume budget.** Add up all of it across the
week — planche lean, tuck planche, front lever, back lever, skin the cat,
support holds — as total seconds under tension. That single number is what your
connective tissue experiences. It does not care which exercise produced it.

**Increase that number by about 10% a week. Not more.** If last week was 400
seconds, this week is 440, not 600.

**Progress seconds before you progress leverage.** Going from 8 s to 12 s in a
tuck front lever is a smaller jump than going from tuck to advanced tuck. Take
the small step first, every time.

**Two straight-arm sessions a week beats four.** Tendon needs longer between
exposures than muscle does. Frequency that works beautifully for push-ups will
hurt you here.

## The warning signs, in the order they appear

1. A dull ache in the inner elbow **the day after** straight-arm work.
2. Stiffness in the elbow first thing in the morning that eases as you move.
3. Pain that arrives **during** the warm-up rather than during the work.
4. Pain that persists at rest.

**Stage 1 means reduce volume now.** Not next week. Halve your straight-arm
seconds for two weeks and the problem usually disappears. Stage 3 or 4 means stop
straight-arm work entirely and get it assessed — at that point you are months from
training normally, and pushing on makes it longer.

## Training tendon on purpose

Tendon responds to **slow, heavy, sustained** load far better than to fast or
explosive load:

- **Long holds** at a rung you own comfortably — 20–30 s, not 5 s of maximum
  effort. Time under tension is the stimulus.
- **Slow eccentrics** — 5–8 seconds of lowering through a position, which loads
  the tissue heavily with less joint stress than a maximal hold.
- **Isometrics at multiple angles** — hold the position at a few points through
  the range, not only the hardest one.

Notice what is missing: explosive straight-arm work. Kipping into a lever,
dropping suddenly into a german hang, bouncing at the bottom of a skin the cat.
Those load tendon fast and unpredictably, which is exactly what it tolerates
worst.

## The supporting work

Two small things, done consistently, prevent most straight-arm elbow problems:

**Slow supination and pronation with a light weight** — 2×15 each way, twice a
week. Loads the biceps tendon in its rotational role, gently.

**Wrist curls and reverse wrist curls** — 2×15 each, twice a week. The forearm
muscles cross the elbow, and a strong forearm changes what the elbow experiences.

Neither is exciting. Both take four minutes. They are the cheapest insurance in
this course.

## A sane weekly straight-arm budget

| Experience | Sessions/week | Total seconds under tension/week |
|---|---|---|
| First 3 months | 2 | 200–350 s |
| 3–12 months | 2 | 350–600 s |
| Beyond a year | 2–3 | 600–900 s |

Those are guidelines, not laws — but if you are well above the band for your
experience and your elbows are talking to you, you have found your answer.

## Self-check

- [ ] I can state my total straight-arm seconds for last week
- [ ] I increase that number by about 10% a week, not by feel
- [ ] Supination and wrist work happen twice a week
- [ ] I know the four warning stages and what stage 1 means

<!-- FILE: 03-static-pull/01-german-hang.md -->
This module is the pulling half of the static track, and it starts with a
position rather than a skill — because everything that follows requires your
shoulders to go somewhere most people's shoulders have never been.

## What it is

Hanging from a bar or rings, arms straight, rotating backward until your body
hangs on the far side and your shoulders are in **deep extension** — arms behind
your torso, chest facing away.

## Why it comes first

Skin the cat, the back lever and every inverted transition pass through this
position. If your shoulders lack the range, you do not skip the position — your
body finds it anyway, under load, with no control. That is precisely how people
hurt themselves.

The german hang is where you earn that range slowly and deliberately, with almost
no torque involved.

## The physics, and the specific risk

In extension, the head of the humerus sits forward in the joint and the front of
the capsule — along with the long head of the biceps — takes tension. Add load in
that position and you are stressing tissue in the shallowest, least supported part
of its range.

That is not an argument against training it. Range you never load is range you do
not really have. It is an argument for **doing it slowly, in control, with your
own bodyweight and nothing else**, and for never, ever dropping into it.

Falling into a german hang from the top is the single most common shoulder injury
in calisthenics.

## The progression

| Rung | What to do | Target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Shoulder extension stretch** — hands behind on a low bar, sink slowly | 30 s |
| 2 | **Bar hang with scapular pulls** | 30 s hang, 10 pulls |
| 3 | **Feet-supported german hang** — low bar, feet on the floor taking weight | 30 s |
| 4 | **Assisted german hang** — toes lightly on the ground | 30 s |
| 5 | **Full german hang** | 30 s |
| 6 | **German hang on rings** — rings allow rotation, which is kinder | 45 s |

Rung 3 is the one to be patient with. Being able to take weight off with your feet
means you can meet the end of your range *gradually* over weeks. Take that offer.

## Form cues

**Go slowly.** Ten seconds to get into the position, not one.

**Straight arms, but not locked hard.** Elbows extended, not hyper-extended or
forced.

**Keep pulling the shoulders down.** Even here, the shoulder is active. Passive
hanging in extension is where the load goes into the capsule.

**Breathe.** If you are holding your breath, you are too deep.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Sharp front-of-shoulder pain | too deep, too fast | back off two rungs, go to feet-supported |
| Tingling in the hands | nerve tension or grip | stop, shake it out, reduce depth |
| Cannot get past halfway | normal early | weeks of rung 1 and 3; range comes back slowly |
| Elbow pain | straight-arm volume, not range | see the straight-arm lesson; reduce the week's total |

## Programming it

Three to four times a week, but treat it as **mobility, not strength**. Two or
three sets of 20–30 s. It should feel like a firm stretch, never like a maximum
effort.

Best placed at the end of a session or on a rest day, when you are warm.

## Target

**30 seconds in a full german hang, breathing normally, no sharp pain.** That is
the gate for the rest of module 3, and it is not negotiable.

## Self-check

- [ ] 30 s full german hang
- [ ] I can get in and out slowly and under control
- [ ] No pinching at the front of the shoulder
- [ ] I can breathe normally in the position
<!-- FILE: 03-static-pull/02-skin-the-cat.md -->
Skin the cat is the german hang turned into a movement. It is the first time you
travel through a full rotation under your own control, and it is the bridge from
"I can hang" to "I can control my body in space while hanging."

## What it is

From a hang: pull your knees to your chest, rotate backward through the bar until
you are in a german hang, then reverse it and come back up.

## The physics

Two ideas, and one of them is new to the course.

**Moment of inertia.** Rotating your body about the bar is easier when your mass
is close to the axis. Moment of inertia scales with the *square* of distance
(`I = Σ m r²`), so folding into a tuck does not halve the difficulty of rotating
— it does far more than that. This is why the tuck version is not slightly easier
than the straight-leg version, it is dramatically easier.

Note carefully how this differs from the static lever rule. In a **hold**, what
matters is d, and it enters linearly. In a **rotation**, what matters is r², and it
enters quadratically. Same tuck, two different mechanisms, two different sizes of
benefit.

**Torque at the bottom.** As you pass into the german hang, your centre of mass
swings away from the bar and the load on your shoulders peaks — at exactly the
point where they are in their most vulnerable position. That is why control
through the bottom matters more than anything else here.

## The progression

| Rung | What to do | Target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **German hang** — prerequisite | 30 s |
| 2 | **Tuck hang** — knees to chest, hold | 20 s |
| 3 | **Half skin the cat** — rotate to horizontal, return | 5 controlled reps |
| 4 | **Tuck skin the cat** — full rotation, tucked | 5 controlled reps |
| 5 | **Skin the cat, one leg extended** | 5 each side |
| 6 | **Straight-leg skin the cat** | 5 reps |
| 7 | **Skin the cat on rings** | 5 reps |

## Form cues

**Slow.** Three seconds down, three seconds back. If you are dropping into the
bottom, you are not training this, you are gambling with it.

**Straight arms throughout.** Bending the elbow turns it into a different
movement and removes the reason to do it.

**Lead with the hips, not the feet.** Think about sending your hips backward
through the gap rather than throwing your feet over.

**Stop before your range ends.** You should finish each rep able to do another.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Dropping through the bottom | too little control for the rung | go back to half skin the cat |
| Shoulder pain at the bottom | insufficient extension range | back to the german hang for two weeks |
| Cannot get back up | usually the tuck opening early | stay tucked until you have cleared the bar |
| Grip fails first | expected on rings; normal | use a bar for now, add hangs |
| Feeling dizzy | inner ear, normal early | fewer reps, more sets, it adapts fast |

## Programming it

Twice a week, 3–5 sets of 3–5 slow reps. Counts toward your straight-arm weekly
budget — at roughly 6 seconds per rep, a 4×5 session is about 120 seconds.

Never do this tired. It is a control exercise, and control is the first thing
fatigue removes.

## Target

**5 slow, controlled tuck skin the cats with a 3-second descent.** Then the tuck
back lever.

## Self-check

- [ ] 5 tuck skin the cats, 3 s down and 3 s up
- [ ] I never drop through the bottom
- [ ] Arms stay straight the whole way
- [ ] Shoulders are quiet the next day

<!-- FILE: 03-static-pull/03-tuck-back-lever.md -->
The back lever is the first true lever in the course — the first time you hold
your body horizontal against a real lever arm.

## What it is

Hanging from a bar, rotated so your body is horizontal and **face down**, arms
straight behind you, shoulders in extension.

## The physics

![The back-lever progression, with measured lever arms](/academy/figures/back-lever-ladder.svg)

Mechanically this is the front lever mirrored: torque about the bar is `m · g · d`,
and the ladder works by shrinking d. The percentages come out the same, because
the arithmetic does not care which way up you are.

**Your shoulders very much care.** In a front lever the shoulder is in flexion —
strong, stable, well supported. In a back lever it is in **extension**, which is
the position you spent the last two lessons earning. That is the entire reason the
back lever gets trained after the german hang and skin the cat, and why it is
usually attempted before the front lever despite loading similar amounts: the
lever arm is manageable, but the joint position is not forgiving.

There is a second-order effect worth knowing: because your arms are behind you,
a small amount of the load is taken by the biceps as a *stretched* muscle. That is
comfortable at a tuck and much less so at full lay, and it is why the last two
rungs of this ladder hurt in a way the first two do not.

## The progression

| Rung | What to do | Target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **German hang** and **skin the cat** — prerequisites | 30 s / 5 reps |
| 2 | **Tuck back lever** — knees tight to chest, body horizontal | 15 s |
| 3 | **Advanced tuck back lever** — back flat, knees away from chest | 12 s |
| 4 | **One-leg back lever** | 8 s each side |
| 5 | **Straddle back lever** | 8 s |
| 6 | **Full back lever** | 5 s |

## Getting into it

From a german hang, **pull your hips up** until your body reaches horizontal.
Do not try to arrive by lowering from the top — that is the eccentric version, and
it is a later progression, not an entry.

Get there slowly, hold, and come down through the german hang with control.

## Form cues

**Body horizontal, not head-down.** Almost everyone holds theirs too steep for the
first few months, which is a shorter lever than they think. Film it once; the
angle is always worse than it feels.

**Shoulders down and back.** The position is extension, not a collapse.

**Hollow, not arched.** The whole body should be one flat plank.

**Straight arms, elbows rotated out.** Aim the inside of the elbow outward. It
protects the joint and keeps the arm honest.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Front-of-shoulder pain | too much lever too early | drop a rung; more german hang |
| Elbow pain | straight-arm volume | week off straight-arm work; see module 2, lesson 6 |
| Hips sag | brace | back to hollow holds; drop a rung |
| Cannot reach horizontal | strength, honestly | hold at whatever angle you can and progress the angle |
| Can hold tuck, nothing beyond | expected — the jump is real | use one-leg and half-lay as intermediates |

## Programming it

Twice a week. 4–6 sets, each ending while the shape is good. A set is over when
your hips drop or your arms bend, not when you are out of effort.

Counts toward the straight-arm weekly budget, and this one adds up fast.

## Target

**15 seconds in a tuck back lever with the body genuinely horizontal.** Then the
tuck front lever.

## Self-check

- [ ] 15 s tuck back lever, filmed once to check the angle
- [ ] I get in by pulling up, not by dropping down
- [ ] Shoulders and elbows quiet the next day
- [ ] I can come down through a german hang under control

<!-- FILE: 03-static-pull/04-tuck-front-lever.md -->
The front lever is the benchmark pulling static, and the one this course spends
the most physics on — because it is the position where understanding the lever
arm is worth the most to you.

## What it is

Hanging from a bar, body horizontal and **face up**, arms straight, shoulders
loaded in flexion.

## The physics

![The four front-lever positions with their measured lever arms](/academy/figures/front-lever-ladder.svg)

Torque about the bar is `m · g · d`. The ladder is four values of d:

| Position | d, relative | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| tuck | 65% | knees to chest |
| advanced tuck | 76% | back flat, thighs roughly vertical |
| straddle | 88% | legs straight, spread wide |
| full lay | 100% | legs straight and together |

And the reason a tuck cannot go lower than about two-thirds:

![Where front-lever torque comes from](/academy/figures/torque-breakdown.svg)

The torso is a third of the total and cannot be folded away. Only the legs can
move, so tucking has a floor.

Two things follow that you should actually use:

**The straddle is mostly a leg-length trick.** Spreading your legs does not change
their mass; it shortens how far they reach *along* your body's long axis, which is
the direction d is measured in. A wider straddle is a shorter d. If you are stuck
at straddle, widen it before you assume you need more strength.

**The gap between advanced tuck and straddle is the biggest on the ladder** —
12 percentage points, against 11 and 12 for the others, but it arrives when you
are already close to your limit, where the difficulty curve is steepest. Almost
everyone stalls here. It is not you.

## The progression

| Rung | What to do | Target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Scapular pulls** and a 30 s hang | prerequisite |
| 2 | **Tuck front lever** | 15 s |
| 3 | **Advanced tuck** | 12 s |
| 4 | **One-leg front lever** | 8 s each side |
| 5 | **Straddle front lever** | 8 s |
| 6 | **Half-lay** — one leg straight, one bent to 90° | 6 s |
| 7 | **Full front lever** | 5 s |

## Getting into it

From a hang: **scapular pull first** — shoulders down, arms still straight — then
pull your hips up and back until your body reaches horizontal.

If you start by pulling with bent arms you will end up in a different, easier,
useless position. The scapular pull is what makes it a front lever rather than a
hanging tuck.

## Form cues

**Pull the bar toward your hips.** Straight arms, but actively pulling — the lats
are doing the work, and they need to be told.

**Hollow, ribs down.** An arch here leaks force straight out of the hips.

**Shoulders depressed.** Not shrugged toward the ears.

**Look at your feet, not the ceiling.** Keeps the neck neutral and helps the
hollow.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Body at 45°, not horizontal | strength; also nobody films themselves | film it; hold at your real angle and progress it |
| Hips sag | brace | hollow holds; drop a rung |
| Arms bend | lats not engaging, biceps compensating | scapular pulls, then re-enter |
| Shoulder pinch | shrugging | depress the shoulders before the pull |
| Stuck between advanced tuck and straddle | the steepest step on the ladder | one-leg, wide straddle, eccentrics from above |

## Programming it

Twice a week. 5–8 sets of a hold you can keep clean. The most useful single
addition: **eccentrics** — get into a rung you cannot hold, and lower through it
slowly for 5–8 seconds. Two or three of those at the end of a session build the
next rung faster than anything else.

All of it counts toward the weekly straight-arm budget.

## Target

**15 seconds in a tuck front lever, body genuinely horizontal, arms straight.**
That is a real strength standard and worth being pleased about.

## Self-check

- [ ] 15 s tuck front lever, filmed to confirm the angle
- [ ] I scapular-pull before every entry
- [ ] Hollow holds for the whole duration
- [ ] I know my current rung's percentage and my next one's

<!-- FILE: 03-static-pull/05-advanced-levers.md -->
The last rungs. From here, progress is measured in months and the difference
between people who get these and people who do not is almost entirely patience.

## What "advanced" means here

Straddle, half-lay and full lay — front and back — plus the ability to move
between them. At these levels the load is close to your maximum, which changes
three things:

- **Volume must come down.** Sets of 5 s, not 20. You cannot accumulate the same
  total time under tension near your limit, and trying to is how elbows break.
- **Frequency must stay the same.** Two sessions a week. Not three because you are
  keen.
- **Eccentrics become the main driver.** Holds maintain what you have; slow lowers
  build what you do not.

## The remaining ladder

| Rung | Front lever | Back lever |
|---|---|---|
| straddle | 88% of full | 88% of full |
| half-lay | roughly 93% | roughly 93% |
| full | 100% | 100% |

Half-lay — one leg straight, one bent to 90° — is the single most useful
intermediate on the whole ladder and most people skip it. It sits almost exactly
between straddle and full, and it is asymmetric, which teaches you to hold the
position without relying on perfect balance.

## Techniques that actually move you forward

**Eccentric lowers.** From an inverted hang, lower slowly through the position you
want. 5–8 seconds. 3 sets at the end of a session. This is the highest-yield
exercise in the whole module.

**Isometric ladders.** Hold your best rung for 5 s, drop to the rung below for
10 s, then the one below that for 15 s, all without letting go. Brutal, effective,
and it accumulates time under tension where it counts.

**Weighted easier rungs.** Add 5 kg to an advanced tuck rather than moving to
straddle. The lever stays manageable while the load rises — and because the load
is added at your centre, it is a cleaner stimulus than a longer lever.

**Band assistance at the hips.** A band under the hips reduces effective d
continuously, which lets you hold the *full* position for enough time to learn its
shape.

## Pulling between the levers

Once you can hold both, train transitions: front lever → inverted hang → back
lever, slowly, under control. This is where the two halves of the module become
one skill, and it is the most honest test of whether you own the positions or
merely visit them.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Stuck at straddle for months | normal | widen the straddle, add half-lay, add eccentrics |
| Elbow pain returning | volume climbed with intensity | cut total straight-arm seconds by half for 2 weeks |
| Can hold it, cannot pull into it | pulling strength, not holding strength | front lever pulls and rows |
| Shape degrades at the end of sets | too close to maximum | stop sets earlier; end while it is clean |
| No progress for 8 weeks | often under-recovery, not under-training | deload a week, sleep more, then reassess |

## Programming it

Twice a week, and a realistic session looks like:

1. joint prep — 10 min
2. one entry drill — 3 sets
3. best-rung holds — 4 sets, stopping clean
4. eccentrics — 3 sets of one slow lower
5. back-off holds at a comfortable rung — 2 sets of 15–20 s

That is 15–20 minutes of actual lever work, and it is enough. The people who get
these skills are not the ones doing more; they are the ones still doing it in
three years.

## Target

**Straddle front lever, 8 seconds, clean.** Beyond that you are in territory where
this course stops giving you rungs and starts giving you principles — which is as
it should be.

## Self-check

- [ ] I train levers exactly twice a week
- [ ] Eccentrics are in every lever session
- [ ] My straight-arm weekly total is written down
- [ ] I stop sets while the shape is still good
<!-- FILE: 04-dynamic-push/01-push-up-progression.md -->
The push-up is the most under-rated exercise in calisthenics because everybody
assumes they can already do it. Most people cannot do a good one, and the good one
is a genuinely useful strength exercise well past the beginner stage.

## What a real push-up is

- body in **one straight line** from heel to head — no sagging hips, no piked hips
- hands roughly under the shoulders, **elbows tracking at about 45°** from the
  torso, not flared to 90°
- chest descends to **fist height** off the floor
- **full lockout** at the top, shoulder blades allowed to spread
- no bouncing, no head-bobbing

## The physics

![Fraction of bodyweight on the hands, by push-up variation](/academy/figures/pushup-load.svg)

A push-up is a lever with your toes as the pivot and your hands as the support.
Your hands do not carry your whole weight — they carry the share determined by
where your centre of mass sits between your toes and your hands.

That is roughly **64% of bodyweight at the top of a standard push-up, and about
75% at the bottom** — the numbers move because your shoulders travel forward
relative to your hands as you descend.

Two useful consequences:

**Raising your hands moves the load off them.** Hands on a wall is about 28%;
hands on a low box is about 55%. This is a continuous dial, exactly like the
planche lean, and it means you never need to do a bad push-up — there is always a
height where you can do a good one.

**Raising your feet moves the load on.** Feet elevated 30 cm is about 74% at the
top, which is roughly what the *bottom* of a flat push-up asks. This is how you
keep progressing without adding reps forever.

## The progression

| Rung | What to do | Target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Wall push-up** | 3×15 |
| 2 | **Incline push-up** — hands waist-height | 3×12 |
| 3 | **Knee push-up** — hips straight, not sitting back | 3×12 |
| 4 | **Negative push-up** — 5 s lower, knees to return | 3×5 |
| 5 | **Full push-up** | 3×8 |
| 6 | **Full push-up, paused** — 2 s at the bottom | 3×8 |
| 7 | **Feet-elevated push-up** | 3×8 |
| 8 | **Diamond push-up** | 3×8 |
| 9 | **Archer push-up** | 3×5 each side |
| 10 | **Pseudo-planche push-up** | 3×5 |

Do not skip rung 6. A paused push-up removes the stretch reflex at the bottom,
which is where most of the "free" reps come from. If your paused numbers are far
below your normal ones, you have been bouncing.

## Form cues

**Push the floor away at the top.** Let the shoulder blades spread. Most people
lock the elbows and stop there; the last few centimetres come from the scapula and
they are worth having — that is the serratus, and it is the muscle the planche
will later demand.

**Elbows at 45°.** Flaring to 90° puts the shoulder in its least stable position.
Tucking them to 0° turns it into a triceps exercise.

**Squeeze the glutes.** The most reliable way to stop the hips sagging.

**Head neutral.** Chin tucked slightly. A head that reaches for the floor first is
adding a rep that never happened.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Hips sag | brace | glutes; go back to knee push-ups and hold the line |
| Hips pike up | avoiding the hard part | one line from heel to head, drop a rung |
| Elbows flare wide | habit | consciously tuck to 45°, lighten the rung |
| Head reaches first | cheating range | film one set from the side |
| Shoulder pain at the bottom | depth plus flare | reduce depth to fist height, fix elbow path |
| Wrist pain | wrist extension | push-up handles, fists, or dumbbells to keep the wrist neutral |

## Programming it

Three times a week, 3–5 sets. Choose the rung where **8 clean reps is hard but
possible**, and progress reps to 12, then move up a rung and return to 8.

Once you are past rung 7, treat push-ups as accessory volume and let dips carry
the main pushing progression.

## Target

**3 sets of 8 paused full push-ups**, chest to fist height, body in one line.
Then dips.

## Self-check

- [ ] 3×8 paused push-ups, 2 s at the bottom
- [ ] Filmed from the side and the line is straight
- [ ] Shoulder blades spread at the top
- [ ] No wrist or shoulder pain afterwards

<!-- FILE: 04-dynamic-push/02-parallel-bar-dips.md -->
The dip is the strongest pushing pattern available to you without equipment, and
the one with the worst safety reputation — almost entirely because of how people
do them, not because of the movement.

## What it is

Supported on parallel bars, arms locked. Lower until your upper arm is roughly
parallel to the ground, then press back to lockout.

## The physics

![Upright versus leaning dip](/academy/figures/dip-lean.svg)

Your hands are the pivot. What changes everything is **where your torso is
relative to them**.

**Upright** — your centre of mass hangs almost directly below your hands, so d is
close to zero and there is very little torque about the shoulder. The work is at
the elbow, and this is a triceps movement.

**Leaning forward** — your chest travels in front of your hands and a real lever
arm opens up. Now the shoulder must produce torque to hold you, and the chest and
front delt take over.

Both are correct dips. They are just different exercises, and the reason to know
which one you are doing is that progressing both at once means progressing
neither.

## Depth, honestly

The often-repeated claim that dips damage shoulders is mostly about **depth**.
Going far below upper-arm-parallel puts the shoulder into deep extension under
heavy load — the same position the german hang trains gently, except now with your
entire bodyweight and a bent arm.

Upper arm parallel to the floor is plenty for almost everybody. Going deeper is a
choice that should be made deliberately, earned slowly, and skipped entirely if
you have any shoulder history.

## The progression

| Rung | What to do | Target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Support hold** | 30 s |
| 2 | **Negative dip** — 5 s lower, step back up | 3×5 |
| 3 | **Band-assisted dip** | 3×8 |
| 4 | **Feet-assisted dip** — bench or box behind | 3×8 |
| 5 | **Full dip** | 3×5 |
| 6 | **Full dip** | 3×10 |
| 7 | **Paused dip** — 2 s at the bottom | 3×8 |
| 8 | **Weighted dip** | 3×5 at +10 kg |
| 9 | **Ring dip** | 3×5 |
| 10 | **Ring dip, turned out at the top** | 3×5 |

Rung 9 is a large jump. Rings add rotational instability, and the shoulder has to
stabilise in a plane that bars remove entirely. Own 3×10 clean bar dips first.

## Form cues

**Shoulders down, always.** Even at the bottom. Shrugging at the bottom is the
single most common cause of dip shoulder pain.

**Pick your lean and keep it.** Decide upright or leaning for the whole block.

**Elbows back, not out.** They should travel behind you, not flare sideways.

**Control the bottom.** No bouncing. The bottom is where the shoulder is most
vulnerable and where the stretch reflex tempts you.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Front-of-shoulder pain | too deep, or shrugging at the bottom | stop at parallel; press the shoulders down |
| Elbow pain | volume or flare | reduce sets, tuck the elbows |
| Cannot get out of the bottom | weak at long muscle length | paused dips at a lower rung, plus negatives |
| Swinging | no brace | hollow body, legs together, glutes on |
| Bars too wide | fixed equipment | find narrower bars — wide bars flare the elbows for you |

## Programming it

Twice a week for the main progression, 3–5 sets. Move rungs when you can do
**3×10 clean** at the current one.

Dips and push-ups on the same day is fine. Dips and heavy planche work on the same
day is not — both are heavy shoulder-flexion load and it adds up faster than you
would think.

## Target

**3 sets of 10 clean dips to upper-arm parallel, shoulders down throughout.**

## Self-check

- [ ] 3×10 dips at a consistent depth and lean
- [ ] Shoulders stay depressed even at the bottom
- [ ] No bouncing out of the bottom
- [ ] Shoulders and elbows quiet the next day

<!-- FILE: 04-dynamic-push/03-straight-bar-dips.md -->
A straight bar dip looks like a parallel bar dip and is not one. The bar is in the
way, which forces your torso to lean around it, which turns it into a very
different exercise — and one that maps directly onto the top half of a muscle-up.

## What it is

Supported on a single straight bar, arms locked, torso leaning forward over the
bar. Lower until your chest approaches the bar, then press back up.

## Why it matters

The **transition** of a muscle-up ends exactly here: chest over the bar, elbows
behind, pressing to lockout. If you cannot do a straight bar dip, you cannot
finish a muscle-up, no matter how strong your pull is.

So this is not an optional variant. It is a muscle-up prerequisite that most
people discover far too late.

## The physics

Because the bar sits in front of you, you must lean your torso forward to clear
it. That lean puts your centre of mass well in front of the support — a larger d
than a leaning parallel-bar dip, and the load lands squarely on the **chest and
front delt**.

There is also a stability cost: on one bar your hands cannot move independently,
so any rotation has to be resisted rather than accommodated. That is why it feels
harder than the numbers suggest.

## The progression

| Rung | What to do | Target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Parallel bar dip** | 3×10 |
| 2 | **Straight bar support hold** — leaning forward | 20 s |
| 3 | **Negative straight bar dip** — 5 s | 3×5 |
| 4 | **Band-assisted** | 3×8 |
| 5 | **Full straight bar dip** | 3×5 |
| 6 | **Full straight bar dip** | 3×8 |
| 7 | **Weighted** | 3×5 at +5 kg |

## Form cues

**Lean over the bar, not away from it.** The chest should finish in front of your
hands.

**Elbows back and close.** Flaring gives the bar nowhere to go.

**Wrists.** Your wrist is in an awkward, extended position here. Do the wrist prep
and keep the grip firm; a loose grip lets the wrist collapse.

**Chest to the bar, not the chin over it.** Same cue as the muscle-up, for exactly
the same reason.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Cannot get past halfway | the lean is not deep enough | more support-hold work at rung 2 |
| Wrist pain | position plus load | prep, firmer grip, fewer reps, more sets |
| Rotating to one side | uneven push | slow down; film from the front |
| Chest hits the bar hard | no control at the bottom | negatives at rung 3 |

## Programming it

Twice a week alongside parallel bar dips — one as the main lift, the other as
accessory. If muscle-ups are your goal, straight bar dips are the main lift and
parallel bar dips are the accessory.

## Target

**3 sets of 8 straight bar dips, chest to bar, controlled.** That is the pressing
half of a muscle-up handled.

## Self-check

- [ ] 3×8 straight bar dips
- [ ] Chest touches the bar, not the chin
- [ ] Wrists tolerate the volume
- [ ] I can hold a 20 s leaning support on the bar

<!-- FILE: 04-dynamic-push/04-pike-and-ppp.md -->
Two movements that point in different directions: the pike push-up leads to the
handstand push-up, and the pseudo-planche push-up leads to the planche. Both are
where your pushing strength stops being about the chest and starts being about
the shoulders.

## The pike push-up

### What it is

Hands and feet on the floor, hips high, body in an inverted V, head descending
toward the floor between your hands.

### The physics

The steeper your hips, the closer your torso comes to vertical, and the more of
your weight lands over your hands rather than being shared with your feet. A flat
pike is roughly a shoulder-biased push-up. A near-vertical pike with the feet
elevated is approaching a handstand push-up.

It is, again, a continuous dial: **the angle of your torso is the progression.**

### The progression

| Rung | What to do | Target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Pike push-up, feet on floor** | 3×8 |
| 2 | **Feet elevated 30 cm** | 3×8 |
| 3 | **Feet elevated 60 cm** | 3×8 |
| 4 | **Deficit pike push-up** — hands on blocks | 3×5 |
| 5 | **Wall handstand push-up, partial** | 3×3 |
| 6 | **Wall handstand push-up, full range** | 3×5 |

### Form cues

**Head goes forward of the hands, not between them.** Aim the top of your head
just in front of your fingertips; it keeps the shoulder in a safer path.

**Elbows at about 45°.** Same rule as a push-up.

**Keep the hips stacked.** As you fatigue the hips drop and it becomes a
decline push-up — easier, and no longer the exercise.

## The pseudo-planche push-up

### What it is

A push-up with your hands beside your waist rather than under your shoulders, and
your shoulders leaning forward past your hands throughout.

### The physics

This is the planche lean with a push-up added. Your centre of mass sits behind
your hands, so your shoulders carry real torque — and unlike the planche lean,
that torque is present while you are also pressing.

It is the most direct bent-arm assistance exercise for the planche that exists,
and it is significantly harder than a normal push-up at the same bodyweight.

### The progression

| Rung | What to do | Target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Planche lean hold** | 30 s |
| 2 | **PPP, hands at ribs, small lean** | 3×5 |
| 3 | **PPP, hands at waist** | 3×8 |
| 4 | **PPP, feet elevated** | 3×5 |
| 5 | **PPP on parallettes, deep** | 3×5 |

### Form cues

**Lean the whole time.** The shoulders stay in front of the hands at the bottom
*and* the top. If you drift back to a normal push-up position at the top, you have
done a normal push-up.

**Protract and tuck the pelvis.** Same shape as the planche lean.

**Fingers turned slightly out** if your wrists complain. Parallettes solve it
entirely.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Pike: hips drop mid-set | fatigue | end the set; the reps after that are a different exercise |
| Pike: shoulder pinch | head going straight down | aim the head forward of the hands |
| PPP: shoulders drift back | avoiding the lean | mark hand position, film it |
| PPP: elbow ache | this is straight-arm-adjacent load | count it toward your straight-arm budget |
| Wrists complain in both | wrist extension | parallettes |

## Programming it

Twice a week, one as a main lift and one as accessory. Pike work pairs naturally
with handstand practice; PPP pairs with planche lean and should be counted in the
same weekly volume.

## Target

**3×8 pike push-ups with feet elevated 60 cm**, and **3×8 pseudo-planche push-ups
with hands at the waist.**

## Self-check

- [ ] Both movements filmed once from the side
- [ ] Pike hips stay stacked through the whole set
- [ ] PPP shoulders stay in front of the hands at lockout
- [ ] Elbows and wrists quiet the following day
<!-- FILE: 05-dynamic-pull/01-pull-up-progression.md -->
The first pull-up is the most requested skill in calisthenics and the most badly
trained. Almost everybody attacks it by attempting pull-ups they cannot do, which
trains nothing except disappointment.

This lesson is the method that actually works, and the physics that explains why.

## What a real pull-up is

- start from a **full dead hang**, arms straight
- **chin clearly over the bar** — not level with it
- **no kipping**, no leg drive, no swinging
- lower under control to a full hang

## The physics

![The four phases of a pull-up](/academy/figures/pullup-phases.svg)

The pull-up has four phases, and most people only know about two of them.

**Phase 1 — dead hang.** Arms straight, shoulders at your ears. Nothing is working
yet.

**Phase 2 — the scapular pull.** Pull your shoulders *down* while the arms stay
straight. Your body rises a few centimetres. Nothing bends.

![Dead hang compared with an active hang](/academy/figures/scapular-pull.svg)

This phase is the one people skip, and skipping it is why most failed pull-ups
stall a third of the way up. If your shoulder blades are not set before you bend
your arms, your lats are not in a position to contribute and the biceps take a
load they cannot finish.

**Phase 3 — the pull.** Elbows drive down and back.

**Phase 4 — the finish.** Chest toward the bar.

And the reason a first pull-up is trainable at all:

![Relative force capacity by contraction type](/academy/figures/force-capacity.svg)

**You can lower about 1.3× what you can lift.** If you cannot do a single pull-up,
you can almost certainly do a slow negative — and negatives load the exact
movement, through the exact range, at a force your tissue can produce. That is why
they build the first rep faster than anything else.

## The progression

| Rung | What to do | Target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Dead hang** | 30 s |
| 2 | **Scapular pull** — straight arms, shoulders down | 3×8 |
| 3 | **Negative pull-up** — jump up, lower for 5 s | 3×5 |
| 4 | **Band-assisted pull-up** | 3×8 |
| 5 | **First pull-up** | 1 clean rep |
| 6 | **Pull-up** | 3×5 |
| 7 | **Pull-up** | 3×10 |
| 8 | **Paused pull-up** — 2 s at the top | 3×5 |
| 9 | **Weighted pull-up** | 3×5 at +10 kg |

## The first-rep protocol

If you are stuck between rungs 3 and 5, run this for six weeks:

**Three times a week:**
- scapular pulls — 3×8
- negatives — 5 singles, 5 s each, resting 90 s between
- band-assisted — 3×5 with the lightest band that lets you finish
- dead hang — 2×max

**Plus, on every other day:** one single attempt. Not a set. One honest attempt,
fresh, and then walk away. This is "greasing the groove" — frequent, non-fatiguing
practice of the exact skill, and it works remarkably well for a movement you are
on the edge of.

Most people who follow this get their first rep in 6–12 weeks. If you are at
eight weeks and nothing has moved, the usual culprit is that the negatives are too
fast: 5 seconds means five, counted.

## Form cues

**Set the shoulders first.** Every rep. Before the arms do anything.

**Elbows down and back**, not out to the sides.

**Chest to the bar**, not chin over the bar. It changes the whole path and it is
the habit that later makes a muscle-up possible.

**Legs together, glutes on, slightly hollow.** A swinging body is a body leaking
force.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Stall a third of the way up | no scapular pull | rung 2, every session, before anything else |
| Stall just below the bar | the finish | paused reps at the top, plus negatives from the top |
| Swinging | no brace | hollow body; pause at the bottom of every rep |
| Elbow pain | volume ramp, or all chin-ups | vary grip, reduce volume, add the forearm work |
| Grip fails first | expected early | dead hangs, 3×max, at the end of sessions |
| Shoulders shrug at the top | no depression | scapular pulls, and pause at the top |

## Programming it

Three times a week while chasing the first rep; twice a week once you have it and
are adding volume.

Once you can do 10, stop adding reps and start adding load. Endless high-rep
pull-ups build endurance you do not need and elbow irritation you do not want.

## Target

**3 sets of 5 clean pull-ups from a dead hang, chest to bar.** Then archer and
weighted work.

## Self-check

- [ ] Every rep starts from a full dead hang
- [ ] I scapular-pull before bending my arms
- [ ] No kipping, no swinging
- [ ] I can lower under control for 3 s on every rep

<!-- FILE: 05-dynamic-pull/02-archer-and-weighted.md -->
Two ways past 10 pull-ups, and they answer different questions. Weighted makes you
stronger. Archer makes you stronger **unilaterally**, which is what the one-arm
pull-up actually needs.

## Weighted pull-ups

### Why

Beyond about 10 reps, extra reps stop building strength and start building
endurance plus elbow irritation. Adding load keeps the rep range where strength
lives.

### How

| Rung | Load | Target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | bodyweight | 3×10 clean |
| 2 | +5 kg | 3×5 |
| 3 | +10 kg | 3×5 |
| 4 | +15 kg | 3×5 |
| 5 | +20 kg | 3×5 |
| 6 | +25% bodyweight | 3×5 |

Use a dip belt, a backpack, or a dumbbell between your feet — in that order of
preference. A backpack shifts your centre of mass backward, which subtly changes
the movement; a belt keeps the load at your centre.

### Rules

**Progress load, not reps.** When 3×5 is clean, add 2.5 kg.

**Never sacrifice range.** A weighted pull-up that stops short is not a weighted
pull-up. Drop the load.

**Warm up to it.** Two bodyweight sets before the first loaded set. Always.

## Archer pull-ups

### What it is

A wide grip; pull toward one hand while the other arm stays straight and slides
along the bar. You end with your chin over one hand and the opposite arm extended.

### The physics

You are not doing half a one-arm pull-up. The straight arm still takes load — it is
a long lever and it contributes real support — so an archer is somewhere around
60–70% of a true one-arm pull-up rather than 50%.

What it *does* teach is the asymmetry: your torso must rotate and resist rotation
at once, which is the actual hard part of one-arm work and which nothing symmetric
prepares you for.

### The progression

| Rung | What to do | Target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Wide pull-up** | 3×5 |
| 2 | **Archer negative** — pull up centred, lower to one side | 3×3 each |
| 3 | **Archer pull-up** | 3×3 each |
| 4 | **Archer pull-up** | 3×5 each |
| 5 | **Assisted one-arm** — other hand on your wrist | 3×3 each |
| 6 | **Assisted one-arm** — other hand on a towel, low | 3×3 each |

### Form cues

**The straight arm stays straight.** The moment it bends, you are doing an
uneven pull-up.

**Pull to one hand, not to the middle.** Your chin should finish over the working
hand.

**Resist the rotation.** Your hips will want to twist. Do not let them.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Elbow pain from weighted work | load climbed too fast | back off 5 kg, add two bodyweight sets, forearm work |
| Archer: assisting arm bends | too hard a rung | archer negatives |
| Archer: body twists | trunk | hollow holds, slow the rep down |
| Progress stalled at +15 kg | usually volume, not strength | more sets at +10, fewer at +15 |

## Programming it

Twice a week. One session weighted, one session archer. Do not run both hard in
the same session — your elbows will notice within a fortnight.

## Target

**3×5 weighted pull-ups at +20 kg or 3×5 archer pull-ups each side.** Either one
puts a one-arm pull-up in sight.

## Self-check

- [ ] Full range on every weighted rep
- [ ] Archer straight arm stays straight
- [ ] Load progresses by 2.5 kg, not by feel
- [ ] Elbows quiet the day after

<!-- FILE: 05-dynamic-pull/03-muscle-up-transition.md -->
The muscle-up is where most people's training goes to die, and almost always for
the same reason: they train it as a bigger pull-up. It is not one.

## What it is

From a hang: pull, transition around the bar, press to a support hold above it.
Three phases, and the middle one is the skill.

## The physics

![The path a muscle-up actually takes](/academy/figures/muscleup-path.svg)

A pull-up moves you **straight up**. A muscle-up cannot, because the bar is in the
way. Your chest has to arrive **over** the bar, which means your body must travel
in an arc — up and back, then forward and over.

If you pull straight up, you arrive at the bar with your chest under it and
nowhere to go. That is the "I get my chin way above the bar and still can't do it"
experience, and it is not a strength problem. More pulling power applied along the
wrong path just gets you to the same dead end faster.

The other half is **lean**. At the top of the pull your torso must already be
leaning forward over the bar. If you are still vertical when you run out of pull,
the transition is geometrically impossible.

## The prerequisites — be honest about these

| Requirement | Why |
|---|---|
| 8 clean pull-ups | you need the pull with room to spare |
| chest-to-bar pull-ups, 3 reps | proves the pull goes high enough |
| 8 straight bar dips | this is the second half of the movement |
| 20 s straight bar support hold | you must be able to finish |

If you cannot tick all four, you are not ready, and working on the muscle-up will
be slower than working on the gap.

## The progression

| Rung | What to do | Target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Chest-to-bar pull-ups** | 3×3 |
| 2 | **Explosive pull-ups** — pull to sternum height | 3×3 |
| 3 | **Straight bar dips** | 3×8 |
| 4 | **Transition drill on low bar** — feet on floor, rotate through | 3×5 |
| 5 | **Negative muscle-up** — start in support, lower slowly through | 3×3 |
| 6 | **Band-assisted muscle-up** | 3×3 |
| 7 | **Kipping muscle-up** — if you want it as a stepping stone | 1 rep |
| 8 | **Strict muscle-up** | 1 rep |

Rung 5 is the highest-yield drill in the whole lesson. Lowering through the
transition teaches the path at a force you can produce, which is exactly the
eccentric principle from module 1 applied to a coordination problem rather than a
strength one.

## The false grip

A false grip — wrist over the bar, bar sitting across the heel of your hand rather
than in your fingers — removes the need to rotate your wrist during the
transition. It makes a muscle-up substantially easier and it is uncomfortable for
the first few weeks.

Build tolerance with false-grip hangs: 3×15 s, three times a week, for a month
before you need it.

On rings it is close to essential. On a bar it is optional but helpful.

## Form cues

**Pull the bar to your ribs.** Not your chin to the bar. This single cue fixes the
path for most people.

**Lean forward as you pull.** The lean starts early, not at the top.

**Elbows rotate through, fast.** The transition is the one part of this course
where speed helps — hesitating in the middle is where it fails.

**Finish with a real dip.** Do not stop with your chest on the bar.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Chin far above the bar, no transition | pulling straight up | rung 4 and rung 5; cue bar-to-ribs |
| Stuck with chest on the bar | pressing strength | straight bar dips |
| One arm goes over first | asymmetry | transition drills slowly, film from the front |
| Wrist pain | false grip, too soon | build false-grip hangs first |
| Huge kip, no control | chasing the rep | go back and earn the prerequisites |

## Programming it

Twice a week, and the session order matters: **transition drills first, while
fresh**, then pull volume, then press volume. A muscle-up attempt at the end of a
session teaches you a tired muscle-up.

## Target

**3 controlled negative muscle-ups through the transition.** Then clean reps.

## Self-check

- [ ] All four prerequisites ticked honestly
- [ ] 3 slow negatives through the transition
- [ ] I cue "bar to ribs" and it changes the path
- [ ] False grip tolerable for 15 s

<!-- FILE: 05-dynamic-pull/04-muscle-up-clean.md -->
You have the transition. Now the difference between a muscle-up that happens and
a muscle-up you own.

## What "strict" means

- starts from a **dead hang**, no swing
- **no kip** — no leg drive, no hip snap
- passes through the transition **under control**
- finishes at a **full lockout** in support
- lowers back down with control

Anything less is a rep, and reps are fine. But strict is the standard worth
chasing, because a strict muscle-up means you own every part of it.

## From one rep to five

Most people get a first muscle-up and then stall for months. The fix is almost
never more muscle-up attempts. It is this:

**Train the three parts separately, at a load above what the muscle-up needs.**

| Part | Exercise | Standard to aim for |
|---|---|---|
| the pull | weighted chest-to-bar pull-ups | 3×3 at +10 kg |
| the transition | slow negatives, and low-bar drills | 5 controlled |
| the press | weighted straight bar dips | 3×5 at +10 kg |

When each part is comfortably stronger than the muscle-up requires, the muscle-up
stops being a maximum effort and starts being a rep you can repeat.

## Reducing the kip

If your reps have a kip in them, take it out deliberately rather than hoping:

1. **Pause at the bottom.** Two seconds in a dead hang before every rep. This
   removes the swing entirely.
2. **Slow the pull.** Not slow-motion, but no explosion from the bottom.
3. **Legs together and hollow.** A leg that moves is a leg that is helping.
4. **Film from the side.** A kip is obvious on video and invisible from inside.

## Rings versus bar

A ring muscle-up is harder in a different way. Rings rotate, so nothing is in the
way and the path is more forgiving — but nothing is stable either, and the support
at the top demands real shoulder control.

Most people find the ring transition easier and the ring support harder. If you
have rings, train both; they expose different weaknesses.

## Programming it

Twice a week:

**Session A — pull-biased**
1. muscle-up attempts, fresh: 5 singles
2. weighted chest-to-bar: 4×3
3. transition negatives: 3×3
4. straight bar dips: 3×8

**Session B — press-biased**
1. muscle-up attempts, fresh: 3 singles
2. weighted straight bar dips: 4×5
3. archer or weighted pull-ups: 4×5
4. support holds: 3×20 s

Attempts go **first**, always. Singles with full rest, never a set to failure.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| One rep, never two | each rep is a maximum effort | make the three parts individually stronger |
| Kip creeping back in | fatigue, or chasing numbers | pause at the bottom of every rep |
| Elbow or wrist pain | false grip volume | reduce false-grip work, more forearm work |
| Great on bar, hopeless on rings | different skill | train rings separately, expect months |
| Transition feels random | not owned, just reached | back to negatives until it is repeatable |

## Target

**3 strict muscle-ups from a dead hang, with control on both the way up and the
way down.**

## Self-check

- [ ] Every rep starts from a motionless dead hang
- [ ] No kip on video
- [ ] Full lockout at the top of every rep
- [ ] I can lower through the transition under control

<!-- FILE: 05-dynamic-pull/05-explosive-pulls.md -->
Everything so far has been about producing force. This lesson is about producing
it **quickly**, which is a separate quality with separate training rules — and the
one that unlocks the last rung of several progressions.

## Why speed is its own quality

Two athletes can have identical maximum strength and completely different
explosive output, because explosiveness depends on **rate of force development**:
how fast you can get from zero to maximum.

For a muscle-up, a bar muscle-up transition, or anything where you must leave the
bar, average force does not matter. What matters is the **impulse** — force
multiplied by the time you apply it — because impulse is what changes your
momentum, and momentum is what carries you through the part where you are no
longer pulling.

This is why a stronger person sometimes cannot do a movement a weaker one can. The
weaker athlete gets to their force faster.

## The rules for explosive work

**Fresh, always.** Explosive work goes first in the session, after the warm-up and
before everything else. Rate of force development is the first thing fatigue takes
away.

**Low reps, full rest.** 3–5 reps per set, 2–3 minutes between sets. This is
nervous-system work, not conditioning.

**Stop when speed drops.** The moment a rep is visibly slower than the one before,
the set is over. A slow "explosive" rep is just a normal rep that hurt more.

**Low total volume.** 15–25 quality reps in a session is plenty.

## The progression

| Rung | What to do | Target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Fast pull-ups** — normal range, maximum speed up | 5×3 |
| 2 | **Chest-to-bar pull-ups** | 5×3 |
| 3 | **Sternum pull-ups** — bar to the bottom of the sternum | 4×3 |
| 4 | **Hands-off pull-ups** — pull high, release briefly, re-catch | 4×2 |
| 5 | **Clapping pull-ups** | 3×2 |

Rung 4 and 5 are genuinely risky if the bar is slippery or you are tired. Chalk,
a dry bar, and an honest assessment of how fresh you are.

## The push side

The same quality on the pushing half:

| Rung | What to do | Target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Fast push-ups** | 5×5 |
| 2 | **Hands-off push-ups** | 4×3 |
| 3 | **Clapping push-ups** | 4×3 |
| 4 | **Explosive dips** — hands leave the bars | 3×3 |

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Reps get slower within a set | set too long | 3 reps maximum, more rest |
| Sore elbows after plyometric work | landing forces | reduce volume; these are high-impact |
| No height on hands-off reps | you are pulling, not accelerating | pull *through* the top, do not decelerate early |
| Wrist pain from clapping push-ups | landing on extended wrists | land on fists, or drop the rung |

## Programming it

Once a week, or twice at most, and always at the start of a session. Treat it as a
small, sharp addition to the plan rather than a workout of its own.

It pairs badly with heavy straight-arm work on the same day — one is fast load on
tendon and the other is prolonged load on tendon, and together they are more than
the sum of the parts.

## Target

**4 sets of 2 hands-off pull-ups**, or **4 sets of 3 clapping push-ups**, with no
visible slowing inside a set.

## Self-check

- [ ] Explosive work is always first in the session
- [ ] Sets end when speed drops, not when I am tired
- [ ] Full rest between sets, actually timed
- [ ] Elbows and wrists quiet the next day
<!-- FILE: 06-programming/01-principles.md -->
You now have the skills. This module is how you turn them into a plan that keeps
working for years instead of six weeks.

## Overload: the only thing that makes you adapt

Your body changes in response to demands it is not currently equipped to meet. No
new demand, no reason to change. That is the whole of training theory, and
everything else is detail about how to increase the demand without breaking.

In calisthenics you have **five dials**, and it matters that you know all of them,
because most people only know the first two:

1. **Reps** — 5 → 6 → 7
2. **Sets** — 3 → 4 → 5
3. **Leverage** — tuck → advanced tuck → straddle. The dial this sport is built on.
4. **Time under tension** — a 2-second pause, a 5-second lower, a longer hold
5. **External load** — a weighted vest, a dip belt

Dial 4 is worth writing down properly, because "go slower" is not a number and a
number is what you need in order to repeat it next week:

![How tempo notation works](/academy/figures/tempo.svg)

Leverage is the one that makes calisthenics work, and it is also the one to be
most careful with, because a change in leverage is a much larger jump than a
change in reps. When in doubt, turn a different dial.

**Turn exactly one dial per week.** Two at once and you cannot attribute the
result — good or bad — to anything.

## The recovery half

Training is the stimulus. **Adaptation happens during recovery.** Train without
recovering and you accumulate fatigue instead of fitness, which feels like
training hard and produces nothing.

The three tissues in this course recover on completely different timescales, and
this is the fact that most programmes get wrong:

| Tissue | Recovers in | What that means |
|---|---|---|
| Muscle | 24–72 h | can be trained 3–4× a week comfortably |
| Tendon and ligament | days to weeks | 2× a week, and volume rises slowly |
| Nervous system | 24–48 h after maximal work | limits how often you can go truly heavy |

This is exactly why the course says **push-ups three times a week and front levers
twice**. It is not arbitrary and it is not caution. They are different tissues with
different clocks.

## Specificity

You get good at what you practise. Pull-ups build pull-ups. Front levers build
front levers. There is transfer between related skills — a strong front lever
helps a front lever pull, a strong dip helps a muscle-up — but it is partial, and
it shrinks as skills get more specific.

The practical version: **if you want a skill, train the skill or its direct
progressions.** General conditioning is good for you and it is not a substitute.

## Fatigue management

Fatigue is not one thing:

- **Metabolic** — the burn during a set. Gone in minutes.
- **Muscular** — soreness, reduced force. One to three days.
- **Connective** — joint ache, stiffness. Days to weeks, and the sneaky one.
- **Neural** — everything feels heavy, coordination is off. One to two days after
  truly maximal work.

The dangerous one is connective, because it accumulates quietly and then presents
as an injury rather than as tiredness. The whole point of tracking your weekly
straight-arm seconds is to see it coming.

## How to progress, concretely

Pick a rung where you can do the target **with clean form and one rep or a few
seconds in reserve**. Then:

- **Week 1–3:** add reps or seconds, roughly 10% a week
- **Week 4:** deload — half the volume, same movements
- **Week 5:** move up a rung, return to the bottom of the rep range
- Repeat

That cycle is boring and it works for years. The alternative — pushing until
something hurts, then taking three weeks off — is how most people spend their
first five years.

## Self-check

- [ ] I can name all five progression dials
- [ ] I change exactly one per week
- [ ] I know why statics are twice a week and dynamics three times
- [ ] My plan has a deload week built in before I need it

<!-- FILE: 06-programming/02-weekly-split.md -->
A plan you follow beats a better plan you do not. This lesson gives you three
templates and the rules for adapting them.

## The rules that apply to every template

**One static focus, one dynamic focus.** At any time. More than that and progress
on all of them slows.

**Statics twice a week, dynamics two or three times.** Tissue timescales, from
the last lesson.

**Skill before strength before conditioning.** Handstands and muscle-up
transitions come first, while you are fresh. Conditioning goes last, if at all.

**At least one full rest day.** Two if you are over 35, sleeping badly, or new to
this.

**Joint prep every single session.** Ten minutes. Never cut.

## Template A — three days a week

The best starting point for almost everyone, and a perfectly good permanent
answer.

**Monday — Push focus**
1. joint prep — 10 min
2. handstand practice — 10 min
3. static: support hold or L-sit — 4 sets
4. dynamic: dips — 4 sets
5. push-ups — 3 sets
6. hollow holds — 3 sets

**Wednesday — Pull focus**
1. joint prep — 10 min
2. handstand practice — 10 min
3. static: german hang or tuck lever — 4 sets
4. dynamic: pull-ups — 4 sets
5. rows or scapular pulls — 3 sets
6. hanging leg raises — 3 sets

**Friday — Mixed**
1. joint prep — 10 min
2. handstand practice — 10 min
3. explosive: fast pull-ups or clapping push-ups — 4 sets
4. static: your current focus — 3 sets
5. dips and pull-ups — 3 sets each
6. hollow and superman — 2 sets each

## Template B — four days a week

For people already training consistently for six months or more.

- **Monday** — Push strength (dips, pike push-ups, planche lean)
- **Tuesday** — Pull strength (pull-ups, rows, front lever)
- **Thursday** — Skill (handstand, muscle-up transitions, explosive)
- **Friday** — Push and pull volume, lighter, higher reps

## Template C — six days a week, short sessions

For handstand-focused training, or anyone who finds daily practice easier to keep
than long sessions.

- **Every day:** 15 min — joint prep and handstand practice
- **Mon / Wed / Fri:** plus 30 min strength, alternating push and pull
- **Tue / Thu / Sat:** plus 15 min statics or mobility
- **Sunday:** rest completely

This one works beautifully for balance skills and badly for people who cannot
stop themselves adding "just one more set."

## A session, in order

1. **Joint prep** — 10 min, never skipped
2. **Skill work** — handstands, transitions. Fresh, low fatigue.
3. **Explosive** — if it is in today's plan
4. **Statics** — holds, levers. Before you are tired; shape degrades fast.
5. **Dynamics** — the main strength work
6. **Accessory** — rows, core, forearms
7. **Decompress** — hangs, gentle stretching — 5 min

## How to fit the course into it

Your two focuses come from the assessment. Everything else is maintenance:

| Focus | Where it goes |
|---|---|
| static | step 4, 4–6 sets, twice a week |
| dynamic | step 5, 4–5 sets, two or three times a week |
| everything else | 2–3 maintenance sets, once a week |

Maintenance is real. You will not lose a skill doing two sets a week of it.

## When life interferes

**Missed a session** — do not merge it into the next one. Skip it and carry on.

**Only 20 minutes** — joint prep, then your two focuses, two sets each. Done.

**Travelling with no bar** — push-ups, handstand work, hollow holds, L-sit on the
floor. The static track survives travel much better than the dynamic one.

**Sick** — nothing above the neck rule; if it is in your chest, do not train.

- [ ] My two focuses are in it, in the right slots
- [ ] There is at least one full rest day
- [ ] The session order is prep → skill → explosive → static → dynamic → accessory

<!-- FILE: 06-programming/03-deloads-and-peaking.md -->
Everyone understands training hard. Almost nobody plans the weeks where they do
not, which is why almost everybody stalls.

## What a deload is

A planned week of reduced volume that lets accumulated fatigue drain so
adaptation can show up. It is not a week off and it is not a reward.

The mechanism is simple. Fitness and fatigue both rise when you train. What you
can actually *do* is fitness minus fatigue. Cut the training, fatigue falls faster
than fitness, and performance goes **up**.

That is why people come back from a deload stronger. Nothing was gained during the
easy week. It was gained weeks earlier and was hidden under fatigue.

## When

**Every fourth week**, planned in advance. Or sooner, if two or more of these are
true:

- performance down for more than a week
- resting heart rate up several beats
- sleep worse than usual
- joints achy in the morning
- motivation gone
- irritable with people who do not deserve it

The last two are real signals, not character flaws.

## How

**Halve your sets. Keep the movements and the intensity.**

- 4 sets of 8 push-ups → 2 sets of 8
- 5 sets of 15 s tuck lever → 2 or 3 sets of 15 s
- keep joint prep exactly as it is
- keep handstand practice — it is skill, not load

Do **not** turn a deload week into a "different training" week. A week of running
instead of a week of levers is not a deload; it is the same fatigue wearing a
different hat.

## Peaking for a test

If you want a maximum — a first muscle-up, a heaviest weighted pull-up, a best
front lever hold — plan three weeks back:

- **Week −3:** normal training
- **Week −2:** volume down 30%, intensity the same
- **Week −1:** volume down 60%, one or two sharp sessions only
- **Test day:** fresh, warm, no new variables

Most people test at the end of a hard block, tired, and conclude they made no
progress. They made the progress. They tested it under a pile of fatigue.

## Testing properly

- same time of day, same warm-up, same equipment
- rested — two easy days beforehand
- one attempt at the maximum, not five
- film it
- write it down

**Every six weeks.** More often is measuring noise.

## Long-term structure

A sensible year:

| Block | Length | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Accumulation | 3 weeks | volume, adding sets and seconds |
| Deload | 1 week | half volume |
| Intensification | 3 weeks | harder rungs, lower volume |
| Deload | 1 week | half volume |
| Test | 1 week | peak and measure |

Nine weeks. Four cycles fits a year with a fortnight spare for life.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "Deloads make me lose progress" | did nothing at all instead of half | keep the movements, halve the sets |
| Never deloading | feeling fine | the week you feel you need one, you needed it a fortnight ago |
| Testing every session | impatience | six weeks; write the date down |
| Progress stalls every 6–8 weeks | no deload in the plan | add one every fourth week |

## Self-check

- [ ] Deload weeks are in my calendar, in advance
- [ ] A deload means half the sets, same movements
- [ ] Testing is every six weeks, rested, filmed
- [ ] I know the signals that mean deload early

<!-- FILE: 06-programming/04-injury-proofing.md -->
Most calisthenics injuries are not accidents. They are volume errors with a delay
built in, which is what makes them feel like accidents.

This lesson is the maintenance work that keeps you training. Four minutes a
session, twice a week, and it is the highest-return time in the whole course.

## Wrists

**Why they fail.** Handstands and planche work put your entire bodyweight through
a joint that has spent your adult life holding a phone. The load arrives at full
intensity from the first session, because bodyweight is not adjustable.

**Prevention**
- the full wrist prep before every session — not the short version
- **parallettes** for pressing work when you can; a neutral wrist removes the
  problem entirely
- build handstand volume in **many short sessions**, not a few long ones
- 2×15 wrist curls and 2×15 reverse wrist curls, twice a week

**If it hurts already.** Stop loaded wrist extension. Work on fists and
parallettes. Keep the pain-free range moving daily. Rebuild volume from about
half of where you were.

## Elbows

**Why they fail.** Straight-arm work loads tendon at length, and tendon adapts
slowly. Golfer's elbow — pain on the inner side — is the signature injury of this
sport, and it is nearly always a volume ramp rather than a technique fault.

**Prevention**
- track your **total weekly straight-arm seconds** and hold the increase near 10%
- 2×15 slow supination and pronation with a light weight, twice a week
- vary grip: pull-ups, chin-ups, neutral, rings
- never do maximum straight-arm work when tired

**If it hurts already.** Stop straight-arm work completely for two weeks. Keep
doing slow, light supination and wrist work — loaded movement helps tendon
recover; complete rest does not. Return at half your previous volume.

## Shoulders

**Why they fail.** Two positions do almost all the damage: **deep extension**
(bottom of a dip, german hang, back lever) and **overhead** (handstand,
handstand push-up). Both are near end-range, where the joint relies most on soft
tissue.

**Prevention**
- earn extension range **before** loading it — the german hang lesson exists for
  this reason
- dips to upper-arm parallel, not deeper
- **shoulders pressed down** in every support and every hang
- band dislocates and wall slides every session
- balance pushing and pulling volume across the week; if you do twice as much
  pushing as pulling, expect problems

**If it hurts already.** Avoid the painful position entirely — do not "work
through" it. Keep training everything else. Front-of-shoulder pain that persists
beyond two weeks is worth getting looked at properly.

## The four-minute maintenance block

Twice a week, at the end of a session:

1. supination / pronation, light — 2×15 each way
2. wrist curls and reverse wrist curls — 2×15 each
3. band dislocates — 2×10
4. band pull-aparts — 2×15

## Reading pain

| Sensation | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Muscle burn during a set | normal | continue |
| Soreness 24–48 h after | normal | train around it |
| Dull joint ache next morning | early overuse | reduce volume now |
| Sharp pain during a movement | stop signal | stop that movement |
| Pain at rest | established injury | get it assessed |
| Tingling, numbness, radiating | nerve | stop; get it assessed |

The distinction that matters most: **muscle pain moves, joint pain localises.**
Muscle soreness is spread out and eases as you warm up. Joint pain sits in one
spot and often gets worse as you go.

## The rules that prevent most of it

1. Progress one variable a week.
2. Never skip joint prep.
3. Add volume before intensity, and leverage last.
4. Deload every fourth week.
5. Stop sets while form is good.
6. Two full rest days if anything aches.

Training is a request. Recovery is whether the request gets granted. This lesson
is the short, unglamorous version of what actually matters, with no supplements
and no hacks.

**A note on scope:** this is general training information, not medical or
dietary advice. If you have a health condition, are managing an injury, or are
changing your eating substantially, talk to a qualified professional. Nothing
here is a substitute for that.

## Sleep is the whole thing

If you change one input, change this one. Sleep is when most of the hormonal and
tissue-repair work happens, and no amount of anything else compensates for its
absence. Short sleep reliably reduces force output, slows skill learning, and —
most relevant here — impairs connective tissue repair, which is the slow link in
calisthenics.

The practical version, in order of impact:

1. **Consistent times.** Same bed and wake time, including weekends. Regularity
   matters more than the exact hours.
2. **Dark and cool.** Both genuinely change sleep quality.
3. **No screens in the last hour.** If that is unrealistic, dim everything.
4. **Caffeine has a long tail.** Half of a 14:00 coffee is still in you at 20:00.
5. **Train earlier if you can.** Hard training late can delay sleep onset.

If you are training hard and sleeping badly, **train less**. That is not a
compromise; it is the correct response.

## Eating, at the level that matters

Three things carry nearly all of the effect:

**Enough protein.** Tissue repair needs raw material. Spread it across the day
rather than in one large meal. If you eat plant-based, pay more attention to
variety and total amount.

**Enough total food.** Chronic under-eating is the most common reason a motivated
person stops progressing. The signs are recognisable: strength drifting down,
constant coldness, poor sleep, no appetite after training, injuries that take
forever. In calisthenics there is an extra trap — lower bodyweight genuinely makes
every skill easier, so being under-fuelled feels like it is working, right up
until it stops.

**Enough water.** Dull, and it moves performance more than most supplements.

Beyond those, details matter far less than consistency. There is no food that will
give you a front lever.

## Bodyweight, honestly

Every skill in this course is easier at a lower bodyweight, because every one of
them is `m · g · d` and you are m.

That is a true fact with a sharp edge on it. Losing fat helps, and is generally
good for you. Being persistently under-fuelled to chase a skill costs you tendon
health, sleep, strength and eventually the skill itself.

The honest framing: **do not diet your way to a front lever.** Train for it, eat
enough to recover from the training, and let bodyweight be an outcome.

## Load management across a week

Your tissue does not know which exercise caused the load. It knows the total. So:

- track **weekly straight-arm seconds** as one number
- track **weekly hard sets** for pushing and for pulling
- keep them within about 10% of last week

When life gets stressful, the total you can recover from drops even if your
training does not change. A hard week at work is a good week to train at 70%.

## Warm-up and cool-down

**Warm-up** is in the joint prep lesson, and it is not optional.

**Cool-down** is smaller than the internet suggests. Five minutes of easy hanging
and gentle movement is plenty. Long static stretching after hard training is fine
if you enjoy it and is not doing much for recovery.

## Signals to watch

| Signal | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| Resting heart rate up 5+ beats for several days | under-recovered |
| Grip strength down noticeably | systemic fatigue, a good early warning |
| Waking before the alarm, unable to sleep again | over-reaching |
| Joints ache in the morning | connective tissue fatigue — reduce volume now |
| No appetite after training | usually under-eating or over-training |
| Motivation gone for more than a few days | take the deload |

- [ ] I sleep at consistent times, most nights
- [ ] I eat enough protein and enough food overall
- [ ] I track weekly volume as a number
- [ ] I reduce training when life gets heavy, rather than pushing through

Everything in this course, assembled into twelve weeks you can actually run.

Print it. Write in it. Cross things out and change them — that is what makes it
your plan rather than a document.

## Before week 1

1. Do the **assessment** — all seven tests.
2. Pick **one static focus** and **one dynamic focus** from your lowest scores.
3. Pick a **template** from the weekly split lesson.
4. Put the **deload weeks (4, 8, 12)** in your calendar now.
5. Start a **log**: date, exercise, sets, reps or seconds, and one line of notes.

## The shape

| Weeks | Block | What changes |
|---|---|---|
| 1–3 | Accumulation | add sets and seconds, ~10% a week |
| 4 | Deload | halve the sets |
| 5–7 | Intensification | harder rungs, volume back to week 1 levels |
| 8 | Deload | halve the sets |
| 9–11 | Peak | best rungs, low volume, high quality |
| 12 | Test | re-assess all seven, fresh |

## Week by week

### Weeks 1–3 — accumulation

The job is **volume**, not heroics. You should finish every session feeling you
could have done one more set.

- statics: 4 → 5 → 6 sets, at a rung you hold comfortably
- dynamics: 3 → 4 → 4 sets, adding a rep per set each week
- handstand: 10–15 min, every training day
- joint prep: every session
- maintenance block: twice a week

End of week 3: your total volume should be about 20% above week 1. Not 50%.

### Week 4 — deload

Half the sets. Same movements, same rungs. Keep handstand practice and joint prep
untouched.

You will feel like you are wasting a week. You are not — this is the week the
previous three become strength.

### Weeks 5–7 — intensification

Now move a rung, and let volume come back down to accommodate it.

- statics: move to the next rung, back to 4 sets
- dynamics: next rung, or add load, back to 3 sets
- explosive: add one session a week
- handstand: unchanged

If a rung turns out to be too big a step, use an intermediate — one leg, a half
position, a band. There is always a rung between two rungs.

### Week 8 — deload

Half the sets again.

### Weeks 9–11 — peak

Quality over quantity. Fewer sets, better ones.

- statics: best rung, 3–4 sets, every one clean
- dynamics: heaviest or hardest variation, 3 sets
- cut accessory volume by a third
- handstand: unchanged

### Week 12 — test

- two easy days first
- re-run all seven assessment tests, same order, same warm-up
- film your best static hold and your best dynamic set
- write the numbers next to your week-0 numbers

Then take three or four genuinely easy days before starting the next cycle.

## A worked example

Someone who assessed at: 25 s dead hang, 8 push-ups, 0 pull-ups, 20 s hollow,
15 s support hold, limited shoulder extension, wrists fine.

**Focuses:** static = support hold → L-sit. dynamic = pull-up negatives.

**Weeks 1–3.** Support hold 4→6 sets of 15 s. Pull-up negatives 3×5 at 5 s.
Push-ups 3×8. Hollow holds 3×20 s. German hang rung 1 every session, because
shoulder extension is the gate on module 3.

**Week 4.** Everything halved.

**Weeks 5–7.** Support hold is now 30 s, so move to tuck L-sit, 4 sets of 15 s.
Negatives are easy at 5 s, so band-assisted pull-ups 3×5 plus one honest single
attempt every other day. Push-ups to 3×12.

**Week 8.** Halved.

**Weeks 9–11.** Tuck L-sit 4×20 s, reaching for advanced tuck. Band-assisted
pull-ups with a lighter band. First unassisted rep is likely somewhere in here.

**Week 12.** Re-test. Expect roughly: 40 s dead hang, 15 push-ups, 1–3 pull-ups,
40 s hollow, 30 s support hold. That is a very normal twelve weeks and it is a
real change.

## The log

Keep it stupid and keep it forever:

```
2026-03-14  PULL
  prep 10min
  tuck FL     5 x 12s      (last set sagged - stop at 4 next time)
  pull-ups    4 x 6        (+2 reps vs last week)
  rows        3 x 10
  straight-arm total this week: 480s   (+9% on 440s)
```

That last line is the one that keeps your elbows healthy.

## After twelve weeks

Re-run the whole thing with new focuses, chosen from your new lowest scores. The
structure does not change. The rungs do.

Four cycles is a year, and a year of this — done unremarkably, with the deloads
actually taken — takes most people from "cannot do a pull-up" to "muscle-up in
sight, solid tuck front lever, thirty-second handstand."

Not because the programme is clever. Because it is the same programme in month
eleven as it was in month one.
