Everything so far has been about producing force. This lesson is about producing
it **quickly**, which is a separate quality with separate training rules — and the
one that unlocks the last rung of several progressions.

## Why speed is its own quality

Two athletes can have identical maximum strength and completely different
explosive output, because explosiveness depends on **rate of force development**:
how fast you can get from zero to maximum.

For a muscle-up, a bar muscle-up transition, or anything where you must leave the
bar, average force does not matter. What matters is the **impulse** — force
multiplied by the time you apply it — because impulse is what changes your
momentum, and momentum is what carries you through the part where you are no
longer pulling.

This is why a stronger person sometimes cannot do a movement a weaker one can. The
weaker athlete gets to their force faster.

## The rules for explosive work

**Fresh, always.** Explosive work goes first in the session, after the warm-up and
before everything else. Rate of force development is the first thing fatigue takes
away.

**Low reps, full rest.** 3–5 reps per set, 2–3 minutes between sets. This is
nervous-system work, not conditioning.

**Stop when speed drops.** The moment a rep is visibly slower than the one before,
the set is over. A slow "explosive" rep is just a normal rep that hurt more.

**Low total volume.** 15–25 quality reps in a session is plenty.

## The progression

| Rung | What to do | Target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Fast pull-ups** — normal range, maximum speed up | 5×3 |
| 2 | **Chest-to-bar pull-ups** | 5×3 |
| 3 | **Sternum pull-ups** — bar to the bottom of the sternum | 4×3 |
| 4 | **Hands-off pull-ups** — pull high, release briefly, re-catch | 4×2 |
| 5 | **Clapping pull-ups** | 3×2 |

Rung 4 and 5 are genuinely risky if the bar is slippery or you are tired. Chalk,
a dry bar, and an honest assessment of how fresh you are.

## The push side

The same quality on the pushing half:

| Rung | What to do | Target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Fast push-ups** | 5×5 |
| 2 | **Hands-off push-ups** | 4×3 |
| 3 | **Clapping push-ups** | 4×3 |
| 4 | **Explosive dips** — hands leave the bars | 3×3 |

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Reps get slower within a set | set too long | 3 reps maximum, more rest |
| Sore elbows after plyometric work | landing forces | reduce volume; these are high-impact |
| No height on hands-off reps | you are pulling, not accelerating | pull *through* the top, do not decelerate early |
| Wrist pain from clapping push-ups | landing on extended wrists | land on fists, or drop the rung |

## Programming it

Once a week, or twice at most, and always at the start of a session. Treat it as a
small, sharp addition to the plan rather than a workout of its own.

It pairs badly with heavy straight-arm work on the same day — one is fast load on
tendon and the other is prolonged load on tendon, and together they are more than
the sum of the parts.

## Target

**4 sets of 2 hands-off pull-ups**, or **4 sets of 3 clapping push-ups**, with no
visible slowing inside a set.

## Self-check

- [ ] Explosive work is always first in the session
- [ ] Sets end when speed drops, not when I am tired
- [ ] Full rest between sets, actually timed
- [ ] Elbows and wrists quiet the next day
