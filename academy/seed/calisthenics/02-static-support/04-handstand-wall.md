The handstand is the only skill in this course that is mostly **not** a strength
problem. If you can hold a 30-second support hold you already have the strength
to hold a handstand. What you do not have is the balance, and balance is a
learned control problem that takes hundreds of short exposures.

Which is why the handstand gets two lessons, and why the programming looks
nothing like the rest of the course.

## The physics

![Centre of mass over the base of support in a handstand](/academy/figures/handstand-balance.svg)

One rule: **your centre of mass must stay over your base of support.** The base
is the patch of floor under your hands — about the size of two spread hands, and
much longer front-to-back than side-to-side.

When your centre of mass drifts past the front edge (your fingertips), you fall
over. When it drifts past the back edge (the heel of the hand), you fall back.
A handstand is not a position you hold. It is a **continuous correction** you run
several times a second.

Your corrections come from three places, in order of how often you should use
them:

1. **Fingers** — press the fingertips into the floor to stop going over. This is
   90% of balance, and it is why the finger pulses in your joint prep matter.
2. **Heel of the hand** — press down to stop falling back.
3. **Everything else** — hips, shoulders, legs. These are emergency measures. If
   you are correcting with your hips, your hands stopped doing their job.

## Why the wall first

A free handstand has two problems at once: staying up, and not falling over.
The wall removes the second so you can learn the first.

There are two wall positions, and they are not interchangeable:

- **Belly-to-wall (chest facing the wall)** — the good one. Your body is in the
  correct straight line and you can walk your feet in until you are nearly
  vertical. Use this one.
- **Back-to-wall (back facing the wall)** — the common one. It encourages an
  arched banana shape, because the wall lets your hips sag. Use it only for
  getting comfortable being upside down in the first week or two.

## The progression

| Rung | What to do | Target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Wrist prep** — from module 1 | done every session |
| 2 | **Plank to pike** — walk feet toward hands, hips stacking over shoulders | 30 s pike hold |
| 3 | **Wall walk** — feet on wall, walk hands in toward it | hands within 20 cm of the wall |
| 4 | **Belly-to-wall hold** | 60 s total across sets |
| 5 | **Belly-to-wall, one foot off** | 15 s each side |
| 6 | **Belly-to-wall, both feet off briefly** | 3–5 s balance |
| 7 | **Heel pulls** — push off the wall, find balance, return | 10 attempts |

## The line

A handstand has one correct shape and three common wrong ones. The correct one:

wrists → elbows → shoulders → hips → knees → ankles, in a **straight vertical
line**, with ribs down, pelvis tucked, legs together and toes pointed.

The wrong ones:

- **The banana** — arched lower back, hips forward of the shoulders. By far the
  most common. It is a shape problem, not a strength problem, and the fix is
  belly-to-wall plus ribs down.
- **The pike** — hips behind the shoulders. Usually fear; you are hedging.
- **The bent arm** — elbows soft. Nearly always shoulder range, not triceps
  strength: if you cannot get your arms fully overhead standing up, you cannot do
  it upside down either.

## Programming it — this one is different

**Frequency beats volume.** Balance is a motor skill, and motor skills are built
by many short exposures, not by long tiring ones. Five or six days a week, ten to
fifteen minutes, is much better than two hour-long sessions.

Do handstand work **first**, while you are fresh. A tired handstand practice
teaches your nervous system a tired handstand.

A good fifteen-minute session:

1. wrist prep — 3 min
2. wall walks — 3 sets
3. belly-to-wall holds — 4 sets, stopping before the shape breaks
4. heel pulls — 10 attempts
5. wrist decompression (fists, gentle stretch) — 2 min

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Banana back | ribs flared, no pelvic tilt | belly-to-wall only; cue ribs down and squeeze glutes |
| Wrist pain after two sessions | too much too soon | halve the volume, more prep, use parallettes |
| Elbows bend | overhead shoulder range | dislocates and wall slides daily |
| Falling immediately every time | correcting with hips instead of fingers | slow down, use the wall, learn finger pressure first |
| Panic when upside down | normal | learn to bail (next lesson) — fear disappears once you can exit safely |

## Target

**60 seconds belly-to-wall in a straight line, and 5 seconds of unsupported
balance from heel pulls.** Then the free handstand.
